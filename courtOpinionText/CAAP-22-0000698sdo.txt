(2) Bekkum argues that the deputy prosecuting attorney 
(DPA) committed prosecutorial misconduct by offering irrelevant 
and unduly prejudicial victim impact testimony by CW and an 
improper closing argument. 
First, Bekkum challenges the State's eliciting of CW's 
testimony that: (1) CW was afraid to make the complaint or go to the police; (2) CW observed that it does not go well for women when they file a complaint; (3) CW was afraid of losing her job if she reported the incidents; (4) CW was scared of retaliation by Bekkum; and (5) CW felt there would be public retaliation if she filed the complaint. As Bekkum acknowledges in his opening 
brief, he did not object to the admission of this testimony or to 
the DPA's closing argument. Accordingly, "'appellate review is limited to a determination of whether the prosecutor's alleged misconduct amounted to plain error' that affected the defendant's substantial rights." State v. Riveira
, 149 Hawai#i 427, 431

n.10, 494 P.3d 1160, 1164 n.10 (2021) (quoting State v. Iuli , 101 
Hawai#i 196, 204, 65 P.3d 143, 151 (2003)). 
Victim impact evidence concerns a crime's effect on
the person harmed by the crime or others, especially the
person's family members. It includes evidence regarding thephysical, psychological, or economic effect of a crime. See 
[HRS] § 706-602(1)(c) (2014) (instructing that victim impactstatements in the sentencing context include "any physicalor psychological harm or financial loss suffered"). 
During a trial, a crime's after-effects are rarely
allowed. [HRE] Rules 401, 403. In State v. Lora , 147
Hawai#i 298, 307-09, 465 P.3d 745, 754-56 (2020), [the
supreme] court disallowed victim impact evidence tocounteract a cross-examination undermining a witness'scredibility. "[The supreme court] reject[ed] an approach
that would permit the admissibility of the impacts of analleged offense on a complaining witness in order to bolsterthe witness's credibility after it has been impeached orattacked." Id. at 309 n.14, 465 P.3d at 756 n.14. 
Id. at 431, 494 P.3d at 1164 (footnotes omitted). 
Here, the challenged testimony was offered to explain 
why CW delayed seven months to report the assaults to the police. 
As noted above, defense counsel emphasized CW's seven month delay by asserting in opening statement that CW was "unbelievable" due to, inter alia , her seven month delay in reporting the assaults. 
Defense counsel questioned CW about this delay in cross-examination. In closing, defense counsel argued that the seven month delay in reporting was "devastating for [the State's] case and for [CW's] credibility." CW's testimony explaining the circumstances and reasons for the delay was specifically offered to counter one of Bekkum's primary arguments attacking CW's credibility. This testimony was not adduced to improperly bolster the CW's general credibility or her factual allegations. Cf. Lora
, 147 Hawai#i at 309, 465 P.3d at 756 (rejecting approach 
that would permit admissibility of impacts of an alleged offense on a complaining witness to bolster the witness's general credibility).

Second, Bekkum argues that the following statement made 
by the State's counsel in closing argument constituted an 
improper golden rule argument that sought to evoke an emotional reaction from the jury and asked it to become an advocate for CW: 
The State asks that you take a stand with [CW], that
you recognize the traumas and fears and the harm inflicted
by this defendant, and that you find this defendant guiltyof two counts of Sexual Assault in the Fourth Degree. 
Bekkum's trial counsel did not object to these remarks. 
We nevertheless must determine whether the alleged misconduct 
constituted plain error that affected Bekkum's substantial rights. See Iuli, 101 Hawai #i at 208, 65 P.3d at 155. 
It is well established that "golden rule" arguments are 
improper: 
Under a typical "golden rule" argument, "a lawyer asks
the jurors to reach a verdict by imagining themselves or
someone they care about in the place of the injuredplaintiff or crime victim." Golden-rule argument, Black's Law Dictionary 807 (10th ed. 2014). These arguments arewidely condemned in both civil and criminal cases becausethey "ask the jurors to become advocates for the plaintiffor victim and to ignore their obligation to exercise calmand reasonable judgment[.]" 
State v. Kauhane , 145 Hawai #i 362, 373, 452 P.3d 359, 370 (2019). 
Examples of improper golden rule arguments include "I say we 
teach [the complaining witness] that there is justice in the world," asking the jury to "send a message to the community that [the defendant's] actions were wrong and would not be tolerated," and "[t]his community is measured by how we treat its weakest members." State v. Conroy , 148 Hawai #i 194, 202-03, 468 P.3d 
208, 216-17 (2020) (citations omitted). 
Here, asking the jury to "take a stand with [CW]" 
invited the jury to base its verdict on considerations other than 
7

the evidence in the case. See id.  Accordingly, the DPA's remark 
improperly appealed to the jury's emotions. 
Prosecutorial misconduct warrants a new trial or the 
setting aside of a guilty verdict where the actions of the 
prosecutor have caused prejudice to the defendant's right to a fair trial. State v. Austin , 143 Hawai #i 18, 39, 422 P.3d 18, 39 
(2018). 
Here, as misconduct has been established, we must 
address the promptness or lack of a curative instruction and the strength or weakness of the evidence against Bekkum to determine whether there is a reasonable possibility that the conduct might have affected the trial's outcome. See State v. Cardona , 155 
Hawai#i 23, 39, 556 P.3d 369, 385 (2024). If so, the misconduct 
is not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, and we must vacate Bekkum's conviction. See id.  There was no objection at trial 
and no curative instruction given. As the case turned on CW's testimony concerning Bekkum's alleged conduct, asking the jury to stand with CW, rather than to decide the case based on the evidence presented, might have affected the guilty verdict. See 
id. at 39-40, 556 P.3d at 385-86 (where prosecutorial misconduct prejudiced the defendant's right to a fair trial and might have affected the outcome, it was not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt). Accordingly, the prosecutorial misconduct was not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

(4) Bekkum further argues that the Circuit Court erred 
in denying his motion for a new trial to allow "newly discovered evidence" in the form of testimony from Bekkum's daughter, Kiki Bekkum ( Kiki). In conjunction with the motion for new trial, 
Kiki declared that during both alleged incidents of sexual assault, she was in Bekkum's car, had a clear view of CW's yard and house, and noticed multiple inconsistencies in CW's testimony compared to her observations of CW and Bekkum. 
A motion for new trial based on newly discovered
evidence will be granted only if all of the following
requirements have been satisfied: (1) the evidence has beendiscovered after trial; (2) such evidence could not havebeen discovered before or at trial through the exercise ofdue diligence; (3) the evidence is material to the issuesand not cumulative or offered solely for purposes ofimpeachment; and (4) the evidence is of such a nature aswould probably change the result of a later trial. 
State v. Stone , 147 Hawai #i 255, 258 n.3, 465 P.3d 702, 705 n.3 
(2020) (citation omitted). 
Bekkum does not challenge or 
otherwise address the Circuit Court's finding that the 
purportedly newly discovered evidence was "just old, ignored evidence" and could not be a "basis to grant a new trial." Accordingly, we conclude that the Circuit Court did not abuse its discretion in determining that Kiki's testimony did not warrant a new trial.
