II. POINTS OF ERROR 
Drapkin raises five points of error on appeal, 
contending that the Circuit Court erred in: (1) failing to rule 
upon and sustain Drapkin's evidentiary objections; (2) denying Drapkin's MSJ; (3) granting DOH's MSJ; (4) denying Drapkin's request for recusal or disqualification of the presiding judge; and (5) prohibiting Drapkin from communicating with DOH. III. APPLICABLE STANDARDS OF REVIEW 
"As a general rule, the appellate court reviews 
evidentiary rulings for abuse of discretion." State v. Acacio , 
140 Hawai#i 92, 98, 398 P.3d 681, 687 (2017) (cleaned up). 
"However, when there can only be one correct answer to the admissibility question, or when reviewing questions of relevance 
under Hawai #i Rules of Evidence ( HRE) Rules 401 and 402, the 
appellate court applies the right/wrong standard of review." Id.  
The appellate court applies the right/wrong standard of review to 
questions of hearsay. State v. Ortiz, 91 Hawai #i 181, 189-90, 
981 P.2d 1127, 1135-36 (1999).

The grant or denial of summary judgment is reviewed 
de novo. See, e.g., Villaver v. Sylva , 145 Hawai #i 29, 34, 445 
P.3d 701, 706 (2019). "[S]ummary judgment is appropriate if the 
pleadings, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and admissions on file, together with the affidavits, if any, show that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law." Id. (citation omitted). 
The grant or denial of a motion to disqualify a judge 
is reviewed for abuse of discretion. State v. Walter , 156 
Hawai#i 65, 73-74, 569 P.3d 1249, 1257-58 (2025) (citation 
omitted). "'Decisions on recusal or disqualification present perhaps the ultimate test of judicial discretion and should thus lie undisturbed absent a showing of abuse of that discretion.'" Id. at 74, 569 P.3d at 1258 (citing 
State v. Ross , 89 Hawai #i
 
371, 375, 974 P.2d 11, 15 (1998)). 
All aspects of an HRCP Rule 11 determination should be 
reviewed for abuse of discretion. Gap v. Puna Geothermal 
Venture, 106 Hawai #i 325, 331, 104 P.3d 912, 918 (2004). "The 
trial court abuses its discretion if it bases its ruling on an erroneous view of the law[.]" Id.

IV. DISCUSSION 
A. Drapkin's Evidentiary Objections Drapkin first argues that the Circuit Court improperly 
failed to rule on his evidentiary objections. This argument is without merit. 
As stated above, when Drapkin inquired as to the 
Circuit Court's ruling on the evidentiary objections, the court orally ruled that the court would not consider statements that were legal conclusions, as those were for the court to make, but that the court would consider factual assertions made in DOH's affidavits or declarations, as well as Drapkin's. 
On appeal, Drapkin further argues that the Circuit 
Court's ruling was, in essence, incomplete because the Circuit Court failed to rule expressly on each of his specific evidentiary objections. However, on appeal, Drapkin makes no specific arguments of error, instead referring this court to his 28 objections made below, the majority of which have two or three subparts, without discernment between the ones arguably falling within or without the Circuit Court's ruling, and without any argument or citation to legal authority. Procedure Rule 28(a) will be disregarded.  Kapiolani Comm. Ctr.

1. Authority to Enter the Purported Agreement 
Drapkin challenges the Circuit Court's conclusion that 
neither DOH nor Deputy AG Armitage had the legal authority to enter into the purported agreement with Drapkin to establish an ambulance base at Princeville, and close the base at K īlauea. 
 We 
hold that, as a matter of law, neither DOH nor Deputy AG Armitage had the legal authority to enter into the purported agreement with Drapkin to establish an ambulance base at Princeville in exchange for Drapkin holding off on filing an UIPA suit against DOH. Accordingly, for the reasons explicated below, we conclude that the Circuit Court did not err in concluding that neither DOH nor Deputy AG Armitage had authority to enter into the purported agreement with Drapkin.

UIPA rights are recognized as important rights under 
Hawai#i law. See HRS § 92F-2 (2012) (stating the important 
purposes and policies of UIPA); HRS § 92F-15(f) (giving 
precedence in the circuit courts to UIPA proceedings). However, the HRS § 92F-15 judicial remedies are clear and discrete, a court can (1) compel disclosure, and (2) order the responding agency to pay reasonable attorney's fees and costs incurred, if the complainant prevails. That's it. Nothing in UIPA or HRS chapter 321 supports a conclusion that DOH has the authority or capacity to agree to relocate an ambulance base in order to avoid or settle a UIPA claim. On the contrary, as discussed above, DOH's duties and authority with respect to ambulance location must be exercised only in accordance with and pursuant to the overall public health mandates set forth in HRS chapter 321. 
It is for these reasons that we hold that, as a matter 
of law, DOH did not have the capacity or legal authority to enter into the purported agreement with Drapkin to establish an ambulance base at Princeville in exchange for Drapkin holding off on filing an UIPA suit against DOH. 
Further, we recognize that the Department of the 
Attorney General has broad authority to provide legal services to State departments, including DOH, which includes the authority to settle imminent litigation against the State. See HRS § 26-7

(2020); Island-Gentry Joint Venture v. Bd. of Land & Nat. Res. , 
57 Haw. 259, 264-65, 554 P.2d 761, 765-66 (1976). However, the 
Department of the Attorney General's broad authority did not grant it the capacity or legal authority to enter into the purported agreement with Drapkin to establish an ambulance base at Princeville in exchange for Drapkin holding off on filing an UIPA suit against DOH, as the Department of the Attorney General (acting through its deputy) must exercise its powers to act in the interests of the public at large, which in this case are expressed in and limited by the mandates of HRS chapter 321. See

Hawai#i

Accordingly, Deputy AG Armitage did not have the capacity or legal authority to enter into the purported agreement with Drapkin to establish an ambulance base at Princeville in exchange for Drapkin holding off on filing an UIPA suit against DOH. 
Therefore, for these reasons, we conclude that the 
Circuit Court did not err in granting summary judgment in favor of DOH, and denying summary judgment to Drapkin, on Count I of the First Amended Complaint.
