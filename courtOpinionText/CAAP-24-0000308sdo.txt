(1) Wife first contends the family court erred by 
finding that Section 2 of the Agreement, "in its plain language, 
. . 
. clearly exclude[d] the [Kāneʻ ohe P]roperty from the 
[Agreement] by stating the property 'shall not be affected by 
this Agreement.'"  Wife asserts the family court read Section 2 
out of context and neglected to consider Sections 1 and 3 of the 
Agreement.   
Principles of contract law govern the enforceability 
of premarital agreements.  E.g., Lewis v. Lewis, 69 Haw. 497, 
500-01, 748 P.2d 1362, 1365-66 (1988).  "To determine whether 
ambiguity exists" in a contract, the supreme court has explained 
that "the test lies not necessarily in the presence of 
particular ambiguous words or phrases but rather in the purport NOT FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAIʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
 
8 
 of the document itself, whether or not particular words or 
phrases in themselves be uncertain or doubtful in meaning."  
Found. Int'l, Inc. v. E.T. Ige Constr., Inc., 102  487, Hawaiʻi
496-97, 78 P.3d 23, 32-33 (2003) (citation modified).  "A court 
should look no further than the four corners of the document to 
determine whether an ambiguity exists."  Id. at 497, 78 P.3d at 
33 (citation modified).  Importantly, disagreement between the parties "as to the meaning of a contract or its terms does not 
render clear language ambiguous."  Id. (citation modified). 
Here, looking no further than the four corners of the 
Agreement and reading all of the relevant provisions together, 
Section 2 is not ambiguous.  See Found. Int'l, Inc., 102 Hawaiʻ i 
at 497, 78 P.3d at 33.  Section 1 begins with the proviso, 
"Except as otherwise provided in this Agreement," before stating 
that the parties' property — both present (as identified in 
Exhibits A and B) and future — would "remain and be their 
separate property," and neither party would acquire an interest 
in the other's property during the marriage.  (Emphasis added.) 
Section 2 then provided otherwise by stating, "It is 
the intention of the parties that the residence presently owned 
by [Wife] located at 45-744 Kamehameha Hwy, [Kāneʻ ohe], [ ] 
96744, shall not be affected by this Agreement."  (Emphasis Hawaiʻi
added.)  And though Section 2's remaining sentence states the 
Kāneʻohe Property would remain in Wife's "sole ownership," that NOT FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAIʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
 
9 
 provision does not require Husband to forfeit his interest in 
any earnings or accumulations arising from the Kāneʻ ohe Property 
during the marriage.  In fact, Section 3 explicitly provides, 
"Each of the parties understand that except for this Agreement, 
such earning[s] []and accumulations of the other throughout the 
marriage would be joint property."  (Emphasis added.)  Because 
Section 2 excepted the Kāneʻ ohe Property from the Agreement, any 
earnings and accumulations arising from the Kāneʻ ohe Property 
during the marriage were, pursuant to Section 3, joint property. 
Moreover, the family court determined that Wife and 
Husband "had different understandings of the provision."  But this alone does not render otherwise clear language ambiguous.  
See Found. Int'l, Inc., 102 
Hawai
ʻi at 497, 78 P.3d at 33. 
Because substantial evidence supports the family 
court's determination that Section 2 of the Agreement was not 
ambiguous, the family court did not err.  See LC v. MG, 143 
Hawaiʻi 302, 310, 430 P.3d 400, 408 (2018). 
(2)
 Wife next contends the family court "erroneously 
denied" her January 29, 2024 Motion for Reconsideration.   
It is well-settled that the "purpose of a motion for 
reconsideration is to allow the parties to present new evidence and/or arguments that could not have been presented during the 
earlier adjudicated motion," and not to "relitigate old matters 
or to raise arguments or evidence that could and should have NOT FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAIʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
 
10 
 been brought during the earlier proceeding."  Tagupa v. Tagupa, 
108 Hawaiʻ i 459, 465, 121 P.3d 924, 930 (App. 2005). 
Here, Wife's January 29, 2024 Motion for 
Reconsideration attempted to relitigate whether she was entitled 
to credits for her contributions to the Kāneʻ ohe Property.  But, 
as Wife acknowledges in her opening brief, the family court, 
having been "presented with ample evidence" regarding her and Husband's contributions, determined that the parties' 
"contribution of labor and payment in the [Kāneʻ ohe
 Property]'s 
renovations" did not entitle either party to credits, because 
those contributions were "the parties' agreement for division of 
labor for their household."  Thus, the family court specifically 
considered and entered findings on the arguments and evidence 
that Wife contends merited reconsideration. 
Accordingly, the family court did not abuse its 
discretion by denying Wife's January 29, 2024 Motion for 
Reconsideration.

 The doctrine of judicial estoppel operates to prevent 
parties from "maintain[ing] inconsistent positions or to take a 
position in regard to a matter which is directly contrary to, or 
inconsistent with, one previously assumed by [them], at least 
where [they] had, or [were] chargeable with, full knowledge of 
the facts, and another will be prejudiced by [their] action[s]."  
Roxas v. Marcos, 89 Hawaiʻ i 91, 124, 969 P.2d 1209, 1242 (1998) 
(quoting Rosa v. CWJ Contractors, Ltd., 4 Haw. App. 210, 218, 
664 P.2d 745, 751 (App. 1983)). 
Here, Husband's aim to enforce Section 2 of the 
Agreement as written and thereby obtain his one-half interest in 
the Kāneʻ ohe Prop
erty's increase in value during the marriage is 
not inconsistent with his position that, in dividing assets, Wife was and should remain the sole owner of the Kāneʻ ohe 
Property.  See Roxas, 89
 Hawaiʻi at 124, 969 P.2d at 1242. 
The record indicates Husband has consistently 
(1) maintained that Wife should receive the Kāneʻ ohe Property and 
(2) claimed some interest in the Kāneʻ ohe Property, be it 
reimbursement for his renovation work or an equalization 
payment. 
Moreover, following an August 2023 settlement 
conference, Wife and Husband "stipulate[d] and agree[d] that" 
Wife would be "awarded her interest in the [Kāneʻ ohe Property] as NOT FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAIʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
 
12 
 her sole and separate property," but "whether [Husband] ha[d] an 
interest in the [Kāneʻ ohe] Property and the value of that 
interest (if any) [were] issues for trial."  Section VI of the 
October 9, 2023 Stipulation, titled "Other Disputed Issues Which 
Are Not Resolved," reiterated that "whether [Husband] is 
entitled to a payment for renovations to the [Kāneʻ ohe] Property 
in 2013-2014 and/or a property equalization payment related to the [Kāneʻ ohe] Property" remains an unresolved issue to be 
determined at trial.  (Formatting altered.) 
In other words, Wife stipulated that the issue was one 
for trial; now that the trial has occurred and the family court 
has determined that Husband was entitled to a property 
equalization payment, Wife cannot challenge the terms to which 
she stipulated.  See Roxas, 89 Hawaiʻi at 124, 969 P.2d at 1242. 
Considering "the family court possesses wide 
discretion in making its decisions," we decline to hold the 
family court abused its discretion by amending Pretrial Order 
No. 1.  See Fisher v. Fisher, 111 Hawaiʻi 41, 46, 137 P.3d 355, 
360 (2006).

 HRAP Rule 28(b)(7) requires appellants to present an 
"argument, containing the contentions of the appellant on the 
points presented and the reasons therefor, with citations to the 
authorities, statutes and parts of the record relied on."  
Without the above, "[p]oints not argued may be deemed waived."  
HRAP Rule 28(b)(7).  And, as Hawaiʻ i appellate courts have 
consistently held, "an appellant's mere challenge of a finding 
does not place the onus of justifying it on this court.  The 
party seeking to overthrow findings has the burden of pointing 
out specifically wherein the findings are clearly erroneous."  
E.g., Tone v. Hashimoto, 156 
Hawaiʻi 299, 574 P.3d 319, No. CAAP-
21-0000324, 2025 WL 2418487, at *3 (App. Aug. 21, 2025) 
(citation modified).

authorities" relied on.  See HRAP Rule 28(b)(7) (emphasis
