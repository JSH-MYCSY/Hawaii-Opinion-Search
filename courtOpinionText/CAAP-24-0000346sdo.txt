Upon careful review of the record and the briefs 
submitted by the parties, and having given due consideration to the arguments advanced and the issues raised, we resolve Rohr's points of error as follows: 
(1) Rohr argues that the Circuit Court erred by 
treating the Motions to Dismiss as "tolling" motions with respect to Appellees' deadline to file an answer to the Amended Complaint. In short, when the Circuit Court granted Rohr's request to file an amended complaint, rather than requiring the Appellees to file new – and likely substantially similar – motions to dismiss the Amended Complaint, the Circuit Court essentially kept Appellees' existing Motions to Dismiss pending

Rohr raised no objection to this procedure. It is

Accordingly, we conclude that Rohr's argument that the

(2) Rohr argues that the Circuit Court erred in 
applying the primary jurisdiction doctrine and dismissing the Amended Complaint. 
Primary jurisdiction "comes into play whenever 
enforcement of the claim requires the resolution of issues which, under the regulatory scheme have been placed within the special competence of an administrative body." Pac. Lightnet, Inc. v. 
Time Warner Telecom, Inc.
, 131 Hawai#i 257, 268, 318 P.3d 97, 108 
(2013) (cleaned up). While "conceptually analogous" to the exhaustion of administrative remedies doctrine, the primary jurisdiction doctrine "
does not  require a determination that the 
court lacks jurisdiction over the matter ." Id. at 268-69, 318 
P.3d at 108-09 (citations omitted; emphasis in original). "Instead, primary jurisdiction presumes that the claim at issue is originally cognizable by both the court and  the agency." Id. 
at 269, 318 P.3d at 109 (emphasis in original) (citing Aged Hawaiians v. Hawaiian Homes Comm'n
, 78 Hawai#i 192, 202, 891 P.2d 
279, 289 (1995)).

(3) Rohr further argues that, even if the Circuit

Appeals promulgated its decision, the statute of limitations for

The Planning Department argues that the running of a 
statute of limitations is not per se an unfair disadvantage. 
Both the Planning Department and the Hui argue that Rohr could have raised the HEPA issue in her petition to the Board of Appeals, but chose not to do so. Regarding the latter argument, HRS § 343-7(a) expressly provides for a judicial action and the Appellees provide no authority for the proposition that the statutory right of action is foreclosed if the issue could have been raised in an agency proceeding. Regarding the former argument, under the circumstances of this case, we conclude that Rohr was in fact disadvantaged by the dismissal of the Circuit Court action "without prejudice," as prejudice effectively attached when the case was dismissed because any newly filed suit would be untimely under HRS § 343-7(a). See generally Fratinardo v. Emps.' Ret. Sys. of State of Haw., 121 
Hawai#i 462, 469, 220 
P.3d 1043, 1050 (App. 2009) (noting federal cases finding that the running of a statute of limitations constitutes a disadvantage). Presumably, Appellees will at least argue that HRS chapter 91 review of Rohr's HEPA challenge is waived in the agency appeal because she did not raise it in her initial petition to the Board of Appeals. Accordingly, we conclude that the Circuit Court's dismissal, rather than the staying of Rohr's HEPA claims, precluded Rohr from having her claims heard on the merits, notwithstanding having properly and timely filed suit pursuant to HRS § 343-7, and constituted an abuse of discretion.
