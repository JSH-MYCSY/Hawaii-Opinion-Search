Defendant-Appellant Cynthia E. Trisolini ( Trisolini ) 
challenges her conviction for fourth-degree criminal property 
damage, in violation of Hawaii Revised Statutes ( HRS
) § 708-823,  
for damaging the plants of her neighbor. She also challenges aspects of her sentence, with regard to the restitution award and the imposition of particular probation conditions. We 
affirm the conviction and the restitution award and vacate in 
part as to the probation conditions.  1

  Trisolini appeals from the April 23, 2024 "Judgment 
and Notice of Entry of Judgment" ( Judgment ), entered by the 
District Court of the First Circuit ( District Court ).2 
On appeal, Trisolini raises four points of error 
(POEs), contending the District Court erred by (1) "failing to 
address whether Trisolini's testimony and Exhibit D proved that 
the plants were on Trisolini's land" and "concluding that property boundaries were irrelevant in determining ownership of the plants, contrary to Whitesell v. Houlton [,] [2 Haw. App. 
365, 632 P.2d 1077 (App. 1981)]"; (2) "failing to make findings 
of fact and conclusions of law regarding whether the plants were within Trisolini's property boundaries"; (3) "awarding 
restitution for costs that were speculative, unsupported by evidence, or unrelated to the damage caused"; and (4) "abus[ing] 
its discretion by imposing conditions of probation that were unsupported by the evidence and unrelated to the offense."

Here, Kreplin submitted a cost estimate from Turf and 
Shrub for five three-gallon ti plants, a papaya tree, and a plumeria plant, which included the cost of delivery and installation. Because the purpose of restitution is to "reimburse any victim fully for losses," which may include the estimated replacement costs of like property, the fact that

Returning Kreplin to the position she was in before Trisolini damaged her plants may reasonably require delivery and installation of replacement plants.  Supplemental Brief9 on this POE requests plain error review, 
citing State v. Kahawai, 103 Hawai ʻi 462, 83 P.3d 725 (2004). 
Trisolini argues that "ordering an alcohol and substance abuse 
assessment and treatment, as necessary, as a condition of 
probation is a deprivation of liberty[,]" warranting plain error review. The State's Supplemental Brief agrees that "this court 
should apply plain error review" to this sentencing issue "for the sake of judicial economy" because an "illegal sentence may be raised by a defendant at any time pursuant to HRPP Rule 40[.]" 
We apply plain error review to this POE. 
A court may impose discretionary conditions on a 
sentence of probation "to the extent that the conditions are reasonably related to the factors set forth in section 706-606 and to the extent that the conditions involve only deprivations of liberty or property as are reasonably necessary for the purposes indicated in section 706-606(2)[.]" HRS § 706-624 
(2014 & 2024 Supp.). HRS § 706-606 (2014 & 2024 Supp.) sets 
forth general sentencing factors a court must consider, 
including "[t]he nature and circumstances of the offense and the history and characteristics of the defendant" in subsection (1). Conditions must be reasonably necessary for the following HRS § 706-606(2) purposes: 
(a) To reflect the seriousness of the offense, to promote
respect for law, and to provide just punishment for theoffense; 
(b) To afford adequate deterrence to criminal conduct; 
(c) To protect the public from further crimes of thedefendant; and

 this record, the District Court did not exceed the scope of its 
discretion with respect to the anger management condition. See
State v. Carroll, 146 Hawai ʻi 138, 150, 456 P.3d 502, 514 (2020) 
(reviewing sentencing matters for abuse of discretion). 
The trial record, however, does not reflect a factual

The District Court did not provide a rationale 
for these conditions, and its purported justification "[b]ased 
on the presentence report" for the mental health assessment condition was inadequate. We conclude the District Court exceeded the scope of its discretion by imposing the mental health and substance abuse assessment probation conditions as 
part of Trisolini's sentence. See Carroll, 146 Hawai ʻi at 150, 
456 P.3d at 514. 
For the foregoing reasons, we affirm in part with 
respect to Trisolini's conviction, and we vacate the sentence 
set forth in the April 23, 2024 Judgment, entered by the District Court of the First Circuit.
