On appeal, Burke contends that the Circuit Court erred 
in several respects by dismissing his claims against the City, 
including by determining that the doctrine of res judicata, or claim preclusion, barred Burke's claims. 
Reviewing the dismissal as a grant of summary judgment, 
we hold that Burke raised a genuine issue of material fact as to whether the judgment in a related federal action brought by the personal representative of Johnson's estate precludes Burke's claims in this action. Under applicable federal law, a party asserting claim preclusion – here, the City – must show (1) an identity of claims, (2) a final judgment on the merits, and (3) privity between the parties. See infra. Burke presented 
evidence of his communications with the estate's personal representative raising a genuine issue of material fact as to whether their interests were sufficiently aligned to establish privity. The Circuit Court therefore erred to the extent it dismissed Burke's claims on any basis that was dependent upon the preclusive effect of the federal action. At this stage of the litigation, we decline to sustain the grant of summary judgment on any other basis. 
Accordingly, we vacate the Rule 54(b) Judgment.

A. Claim Preclusion 
Burke contends that the Circuit Court erred in applying 
claim or issue preclusion to this case "when there's different 
and distinct causes of action and a different party bringing the claims." 
"The doctrine of res judicata, or claim preclusion, is 
founded in concerns of judicial economy to prevent litigants from bringing successive actions based on the same facts." Wong
, 111 
Hawai#i at 477, 143 P.3d at 16 (citing Kauhane v. Acutron Co.
, 71 
7 FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAI #I REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
Haw. 458, 463, 795 P.2d 276, 278–79 (1990)); see Brownback v. 
King, 592 U.S. 209, 215 n.3 (2021) ("Claim preclusion prevents parties from relitigating the same claim or cause of action, even if certain issues were not litigated in the prior action." (internal quotation marks omitted) (citing Lucky Brand Dungarees, Inc. v. Marcel Fashions Grp., Inc., 590 U.S. 405, 411 (2020))). The preclusive effect of the federal action on this subsequent state action is governed by federal claim preclusion law. Wong, 
111 Hawai# i at 477, 143 P.3d at 16 (citing Semtek Int'l Inc. v. 
Lockheed Martin Corp., 531 U.S. 497, 507 (2001)). Under federal law, a party asserting claim preclusion must show "(1) an identity of claims, (2) a final judgment on the merits, and (3) privity between parties." Headwaters Inc. v. U.S. Forest Serv., 
399 F.3d 1047, 1052 (9th Cir. 2005) (quoting Tahoe-Sierra Pres. Council, Inc. v. Tahoe Reg'l Plan. Agency, 322 F.3d 1064, 1077 (9th Cir. 2003)) 
1. Identity of Claims 
"Suits involve the same claim . . . if the later suit 
arises from the same transaction or involves a common nucleus of operative facts." Brownback, 592 U.S. at 215 n.3 (internal 
quotation marks and brackets omitted) (quoting Lucky Brand Dungarees, 590 U.S. at 411). The Ninth Circuit considers four criteria in determining whether successive cases involve the same claim: "(1) whether the two suits arise out of the same transactional nucleus of facts; (2) whether rights or interests established in the prior judgment would be destroyed or impaired by prosecution of the second action; (3) whether the two suits involve infringement of the same right; and (4) whether substantially the same evidence is presented in the two actions." Mpoyo v. Litton Electro-Optical Sys., 430 F.3d 985, 987 (9th Cir. 2005) (citing Chao v. A–One Med. Servs., Inc., 346 F.3d 908, 921 (9th Cir. 2003)). 
The first criterion is the "most important" and is 
often "outcome determinative." Media Rights Techs., Inc. v. 
Microsoft Corp., 922 F.3d 1014, 1028-29 (9th Cir. 2019) (quoting Harris v. Cty. of Orange, 682 F.3d 1126, 1131 (9th Cir. 2012), 
8
   FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAI #I REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
and citing numerous other cases); Mpoyo
, 430 F.3d at 987. 
"Whether two suits arise from the same transactional nucleus of 
facts depends upon whether they are related to the same set of facts and whether they could conveniently be tried together." In 
re Nance, 156 F.4th 961, 966 (9th Cir. 2025) (internal quotation marks and emphasis omitted) (quoting ProShipLine Inc. v. Aspen Infrastructures Ltd., 609 F.3d 960, 968 (9th Cir. 2010)); see Media Rights Techs., 922 F.3d at 1027 ("We are most concerned with the facts or events from which the alleged harms arose."). "Newly articulated claims based on the same nucleus of facts may still be subject to a res judicata finding if the claims could have been brought in the earlier action." Tahoe–Sierra Pres. 
Council, 322 F.3d at 1078. 
Here, the federal action and this state court action 
both arise from the same transaction or set of facts, namely Johnson's death "on the premises" of the Kapolei Police Station following her release from police custody. Specifically, EAS brought claims for negligence and violation of civil rights against the City based on this set of facts.

2. Final Judgment on the Merits 
"[A] final judgment of a federal district court is 
final for the purposes of res judicata even if an appeal is pending." 
Wong, 111 Hawai# i at 477, 143 P.3d at 16 (citing Robi 
v. Five Platters, Inc., 838 F.2d 318, 327 (9th Cir. 1988)). 
Here, the District Court entered a final judgment on 
EAS's federal and state law claims against the City. The judgment was based on the court's: 
(1) November 8, 2023 order 
dismissing the state law negligence claim (Count I) with prejudice and the federal civil rights claim (Count II) without prejudice for failure to state a claim under FRCP Rule 12(b)(6); and (2) December 18, 2023 order dismissing Count II with prejudice after EAS failed to file a second amended complaint. EAS I, 2023 WL 7385604, at *6 ("Count I is dismissed with prejudice because it is clear that Count I cannot be saved by amendment"), *11; EAS II, 2023 WL 8716880, at *1.

Burke argues that the dismissal was "procedural and not 
substantive in nature" because EAS "failed to file a [s]econd 
[a]mended [c]omplaint on time." Not so. "Dismissal for failure to state a claim is a judgment on the merits for purposes of claim preclusion." Save the Bull Trout v. Williams, 51 F.4th

874, 876 (7th Cir. 2008)). 
3. Privity Between the Parties 
"For two parties to have privity, they must be 'so 
identified in interest that they represent precisely the same right' on the relevant issues." Grondal v. United States, 21 
F.4th 1140, 1163 (9th Cir. 2021) (ellipsis and brackets omitted) (quoting In re Schimmels, 127 F.3d 875, 881 (9th Cir. 1997); Washington v. Crown Res. Corp., No. 25-2397, 2026 WL 2903703, at *4 (9th Cir. Sept. 28, 2026) ("Our precedents require precise alignment of relevant interests to overcome the general rule against nonparty preclusion."). 
As to EAS's relationship to Burke, EAS is the personal 
representative of Johnson's estate and Burke is a purported heir. Burke argues that he is a "different party" than EAS, and points out that "[Counsel] on behalf of EAS made it clear that he did not want [Burke] to be a part of the District Court case as he vehemently objected to the filing of [Burke's] joinder via email." Indeed, Burke presented evidence that when he sought to intervene in the federal action, EAS's counsel asserted that Burke was "collaterally estopped" from doing so with respect to any of his asserted claims, and took the position based on a probate court ruling that Burke "[is] not an heir" of Johnson's estate. 
We conclude that this evidence raised at least a 
genuine issue of material fact as to whether Burke and EAS were so identified in interest that they "represent precisely the same right." Grondal, 21 F.4th at 1163; see Crown Res., 2026 WL 
11

2903703, at *5 ("Whether a non-party has been 'adequately 
represented by someone with the same interests is a fact-intensive inquiry.'" (ellipsis omitted) (quoting GP Vincent II v. Est. of Beard, 68 F.4th 508, 518 (9th Cir. 2023))). To the extent the MSJ was granted on the basis of claim preclusion, it was in error. 
B. Wrongful Death Claims by a Sibling 
Under HRS § 663-3 
In support of his second contention (supra), Burke 
disputes the City's argument that as Johnson's sibling, he could 
not bring a wrongful death claim. Relying on Lealaimatafao v. Woodward-Clyde Consultants, 75 Haw. 544, 867 P.2d 220 (1994), he argues that he was "wholly or partly dependent [on Johnson] for physical, moral, and/or social necessities." 
The City's argument – that HRS § 663-3 does not 
authorize wrongful death actions by siblings – was not made in the MSJ. Rather, the argument was first asserted in the City's reply memorandum, giving Burke no opportunity to respond as contemplated under HRCP Rule 56. Because the City's argument was not properly presented under HRCP Rule 56, it could not properly have been the basis for granting the MSJ. 
C. "Derivative" Wrongful Death Claims Under HRS § 663-3 
In support of his third contention (supra), Burke 
disputes the City's argument that his wrongful death claims were 
dependent on the viability of Johnson's claims against the City, which the District Court found not to be viable. 
The City makes the same argument in its answering brief 
that it made below. The argument, however, is dependent upon the alleged preclusive effect of the judgment in the federal action. As discussed above, Burke presented evidence raising at least a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Burke and EAS were in privity, and thus a genuine issue as to whether the federal judgment precluded his wrongful death claims here. To the extent the MSJ was granted based on the City's "derivative" argument, it was in error.

There is no indication that the Circuit Court granted 
the MSJ based on the notice issue, and the City does not respond to Burke's argument in its answering brief. We therefore decline to sustain the grant of summary judgment on this basis. 
F. Findings of Fact and Conclusions of Law 
Burke contends that the Circuit Court erred in not 
issuing findings of fact and conclusions of law. He cites no 
authority imposing such a requirement when a trial court grants a motion for summary judgment. See Hilo Bay Marina, LLC v. State, 
156 Hawai# i 478, 487 n.11, 575 P.3d 568, 577 n.11 (2025) 
("[A]lthough it may be helpful for a trial court to make findings that specific material facts are uncontested
, such findings – 
even if unchallenged on appeal - do not bind this court nor alter our de novo standard of review." (citing 10A Wright & Miller's Federal Practice & Procedure § 2716 (4th ed. 2025) for the proposition: "In considering an appeal from the disposition of a Rule 56 motion, the appellate court normally will not have the district court's findings of fact and conclusions of law on the motion before it since Rule 52(a)(3) makes these findings unnecessary on summary judgment motions.")). Burke's argument is without merit.
