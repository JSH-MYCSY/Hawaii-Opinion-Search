(1) Rapozo first argues "ample evidence in the record

"[J]udicial review, through an HRPP Rule 40 petition, 
of the HPA's decision to deny parole" is limited in scope, and 
Hawaiʻi courts have "recognized the need to preserve the parole

at 222.

A convicted person possesses "no constitutional or 
inherent right . . . to be conditionally released before the 
expiration of a valid sentence." Greenholtz v. Inmates of Neb.

 Penal & Corr. Complex  , 442 U.S. 1, 7 (1979); Mujahid v. Apao, 
795 F. Supp. 1020, 1024 (D. Haw. 1992) ("A proper application of 
Greenholtz  requires the court to conclude that the [Hawaiʻ i]  
parole statute does not create a liberty interest protected by 
the Due Process Clause.").  
Courts reviewing a paroling authority's grant or 
denial of parole may compel the paroling authority to act when 
it is required to, but they "may not compel the [paroling authority] to act in any particular manner since such determinations lie within [the paroling authority's] statutory authority." State v. Schlarp, 541 P.2d 411, 414 (Ariz. Ct. 
App. 1975); see generally 67A C.J.S. Pardon & Parole § 64 (2026) 
("Although the court may compel a parole board to act where it 
is required to act, a court does not have authority to compel the board to act in any particular manner, or to control its discretion, and the court may not conduct its own parole hearing on review of the denial of parole and order the parole board to release a prisoner on parole." (footnotes omitted)). 
Here, the Rule 40 Court found HPA acted arbitrarily 
and capriciously when it denied Rapozo's requests for parole 
based on his failure to complete certain requirements, because 
Rapozo was not afforded "an opportunity to participate in the required programs and work furlough." The Rule 40 Court found in favor of Rapozo and directed HPA to "conduct a new parole

In light of the aforementioned principles, the Rule 40 
Court acted within its authority by ordering HPA to conduct a new parole hearing. See, e.g., Schlarp, 541 P.2d at 414. Thus, 
it did not err by declining to order Rapozo's immediate release or participation in work furlough. 
(3) Rapozo finally contends that, because the Rule 40 
Court "held that the restitution judgment was an illegal sentence," he is "entitled to reimbursement for any money that was collected from him." Accordingly, Rapozo argues the Rule 40 Court should have ordered "an accounting of all funds seized from his accounts as payments of restitution and should have ordered [DCR] to return those funds to" him. 
Here, Rapozo raised this issue before the Rule 40 
Court in his supplemental brief on March 11, 2024. But, the 
January 30, 2024 Evidentiary Hearing (and further hearing on 
April 16, 2024) and May 14, 2024 Restitution Resentencing Hearing transcripts are devoid of any discussion regarding restitution reimbursement. Thus, nothing in the record indicates that the parties litigated the issue prior to the Rule 40 Court entering its Rule 40 Order. 
After entering its Rule 40 Order, the Rule 40 Court

The Rule 40 Court resolved the issues on which the 
case was remanded by the supreme court; to that extent, it did not err. Any issues related to reimbursement of the restitution paid may require factual findings and input by the parties and 
should therefore be litigated in the first instance below. 
Based on the foregoing, we affirm the Rule 40 Court's 
May 3, 2024 Rule 40 Order without prejudice to Rapozo seeking 
relief related to reimbursement of payments made under the illegal restitution sentence.
