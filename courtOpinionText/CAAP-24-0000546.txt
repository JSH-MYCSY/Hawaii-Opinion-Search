OPINION OF THE COURT BY NAKASONE, CHIEF JUDGE 
This appeal concerns whether a sentencing court may 
impose sex offender registration under Hawaii Revised Statutes 
(HRS) Chapter 846E as a discretionary condition of probation on 
a defendant who was not subject to mandatory registration under 
that chapter. 
We hold that a sentencing court may not do so.

II. STANDARD OF REVIEW 
"When a defendant challenges a sentencing court's 
decision regarding the terms and conditions of his or her probation on appeal, the appellate court's inquiry is limited to 
reviewing the sentencing court's decision for an abuse of 
discretion." State v. Anzalone
, 141 Hawaiʻi 445, 457, 412 P.3d 
951, 963 (2018) (citations omitted).

B. Sex offender registration cannot be imposed as a
probation condition where a defendant is not a
covered offender under HRS § 846E-1. 
The parties' appellate briefs reflect their agreement 
that Kauakahi is not a "covered offender" under HRS § 846E-1 and

 therefore not subject to mandatory sex offender registration. 
The parties' disagreement turns on whether a trial court may 
impose sex offender registration as a discretionary condition of probation for a non-covered offender. 
Kauakahi argues that the Circuit Court "had no 
discretion to order [him] to register as a sex offender as a condition of probation[,]" reasoning that the "standardized and comprehensive" nature of HRS Chapter 846E's "statewide scheme" 
precludes its discretionary application, and that HRS Chapter 
846E "reflects the legislature's intentions concerning what categories of offenders should be subjected to the significant burdens of the sex offender registry." Kauakahi relies on the "well-known rule of statutory construction that where there is a conflict between a general provision and a specific provision, 
the specific provision prevails[,]" citing Ho ʻomoana Found. v. 
Land Use Comm'n, 152 
Hawaiʻi 337, 344, 526 P.3d 314, 321 (2023). 
The State responds that under HRS § 706-624(2)(t) 
(2014 & 2024 Supp.), a sentencing court is authorized to 
discretionarily impose "other reasonable conditions" of a probation sentence "to the extent that the conditions are 
reasonably related" to the HRS § 706-606 sentencing factors and 
"to the extent that the conditions involve only deprivations of liberty or property as are reasonably necessary" for such purposes. 
Despite the State's acknowledgement that "a number of 
appellate courts in other states have held that a court lacks authority" to order a non-covered offender to register as a sex 
offender as a probation condition, the State "[n]evertheless 
. . . urges this court to hold that sentencing courts have the discretion to order a defendant to register pursuant to HRS Chapter 846E, when the record indicates that such registration is warranted for the particular defendant."

  1. Kauakahi cannot be ordered to register as asex offender under the general probation
conditions statute of HRS § 706-624, where he
is not a covered offender under the specific
HRS Chapter 846E statutes defining who is
subject to sex offender registration. 
"[W]here there is a 'plainly irreconcilable' conflict 
between a specific statute and a general statute concerning the 
same subject matter, the specific statute will be favored." State v. Spencer, 68 Haw. 622, 624, 725 P.2d 799, 800 (1986) 
(citation omitted). 
"It is the generally accepted rule of 
statutory construction that unless a legislative intention to 
the contrary clearly appears, special or particular provisions control over general provisions, terms or expressions." 
Hoʻomoana Found., 152 Hawai ʻi at 344, 526 P.3d at 321 (citation 
omitted). 
Hawai ‘i
 has a specific statute for sex offender 
registration, HRS § 846E-2, that limits registration to "covered 
offenders," which excludes Kauakahi's convicted offense. See 
HRS § 846E-1. 
HRS Chapter 846E also includes a statute 
specifically requiring judges, inter alia, who release a covered offender following sentencing or probation, to explain the duty 
to register and consequences of such failure "to the covered 
offender," and to obtain "all of the registration information required by this chapter" "from the covered offender." HRS § 846E-4 (2014). 
Hawaiʻi
 also has general sentencing statutes under HRS 
Chapter 706, entitled "Disposition of Convicted Defendants." 
Probation is one of the authorized dispositions for convicted 
defendants. 
HRS § 706-605(1)(a) (2016 & 2023 Supp.). HRS 
§ 706-624 sets forth "Conditions of probation" for a probation sentence. HRS § 706-624 sets forth a list of "[m]andatory 
10  
 FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAI ‘I REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
 
 
  conditions" of probation that a court must impose under 
subsection (1), and a list of "[d]iscretionary conditions" a 
court "may" impose under subsection (2). Seven mandatory conditions are set forth in subsection (1)(a) through (1)(g), and nineteen specific discretionary conditions are set forth in subsection (2)(a) through (2)(s). 
Subsection (2) further allows 
unspecified discretionary conditions in subsection (2)(t), for "other reasonable conditions as the court may impose." The 
mandatory and discretionary conditions set forth in HRS § 706-
624 apply generally to all probation sentences. 
Here, the specific sex offender registration statute, 
HRS § 846E-1, defines "covered offender," and excludes Kauakahi's convicted offense. HRS § 846E-4 specifically prescribes how registration information is gathered from covered 
offenders. It is plainly evident that sex offender registration 
under HRS Chapter 846E is a specific and comprehensive statutory scheme. The general probation conditions statute, HRS § 706-624, cannot be read to subject persons beyond the specific "covered offenders" set forth in HRS § 846E-1 to sex offender registration. See Spencer, 68 Haw. at 624, 725 P.2d at 800; 
Hoʻomoana Found., 152 Hawai ʻi at 344, 526 P.3d at 321. Because 
sex offender registration under HRS Chapter 846E is not 
authorized for a non-covered offender, it is not a probation condition that could permissibly be imposed. 
Our conclusion -- that sex offender registration may 
not be imposed as a probation condition on a non-covered offender -- is consistent with the majority of other 
jurisdictions that have examined this question. Cases in 
Alaska, California, Indiana, Montana, and New Mexico have similarly held that a sentencing court does not have authority to impose sex offender registration as a discretionary condition

of probation for an offense not enumerated in the sex offender 
registration statutes, absent an explicit grant of such 
authority. See, e.g., Whitehead v. State, 985 P.2d 1019, 1021 (Alaska Ct. App. 1999) (holding that Alaska's sentencing and probation statutes "should not [be] construe[d] . . . to allow sentencing judges to impose sex offender registration as a condition of probation when the legislature has not expressly authorized sentencing courts to exercise this power"); In re 
Bernardino S., 5 Cal. Rptr. 2d 746, 752 (Cal. Ct. App. 1992) 
(concluding that "the trial court lacked the power to impose" sex offender registration on a defendant because under the then-existing law, "the Legislature adopted a classification which exempted [the defendant] from the registration requirement"); Straw v. State, 133 N.E.3d 765, 768 (Ind. Ct. App. 2019) 
(holding that "[a] trial court is without authority to order a 
person who has not been convicted of one of the offenses set forth in the statute to register as a sex offender"); State v. Collins, 528 P.3d 1106, 1117 (Mont. 2023) (holding that "the District Court did not have the authority to impose [sex offender] registration as a condition of his sentence" because "courts do not have the power to impose a criminal sentence 
unless authorized by a specific grant of statutory authority"; 
State v. Williams, 141 P.3d 538, 541 (N.M. Ct. App. 2006) (holding that "the district court could not use [sex offender registration] as a punishment tool in its sentence" because the defendant "was not convicted of a sex offense" and therefore "the district court could not statutorily require [him] to 
register"); but see, Loya v. State, 740 S.E.2d 382, 385 (Ga. Ct. 
App. 2013) (concluding that "a trial court may order sex offender registration for felony public indecency as a condition of probation even though such registration is not required by

 the registration statute[,]" reasoning that "appellate courts 
will approve 'any reasonable condition imposed for probation of 
sentence by the trial court in the absence of express authority to the contrary'"). 
2. The State's reliance on cases upholding the
imposition of sex offender treatment  as a 
probation condition is inapt. 
The State asserts that State v. Solomon, 107 Hawai ʻi 
117, 111 P.3d 12 (2005) and State v. Bailey, No. CAAP-14-
0000386, 2015 WL 405742 (Haw. App. Jan. 30, 2015) (SDO) 
"instruct that courts have wide discretion in imposing sex offender treatment as a condition of probation . . . even if the defendant was not
 convicted of a sexual offense." In Solomon, 
the supreme court upheld a trial court's imposition of sex offender treatment as a condition of probation on a defendant 
who pled guilty to abuse of a family or household member and had 
a history of sexual misconduct, 107 Hawai ʻi at 131, 111 P.3d at 
26; in Bailey, 2015 WL 405742, at *4-5, this court likewise 
upheld a trial court's imposition of sex offender treatment as a condition of probation on a defendant who pled no contest to second-degree assault, a non-sexual offense, but whose conduct 
involved improper sexual behavior. 
The State's argument rests 
on an incorrect premise that the imposition of sex offender 
registration  as a discretionary condition of probation is 
analogous to a probation condition of sex offender treatment . 
Such conflation is inapt. 
First, sex offender treatment  and sex offender 
registration  are governed by two different statutory schemes --
HRS Chapter 353E and Chapter 846E, respectively. HRS § 353E-1 (2015 and 2024 Supp.) establishes "the statewide integrated sex offender treatment program for the treatment of adult sex 
13
  
 FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAI ‘I REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
 
 
offenders in the custody of the State[,]" and specifies the 
program's responsibilities. 
HRS Chapter 353E contains no 
provision equivalent to HRS § 846E-2 mandating sex offender 
treatment  for certain classes or types of offenses. Second, 
courts imposing a sentence are authorized to impose sex offender treatment  -- a type of mental health treatment -- as a 
discretionary condition of probation under HRS § 706-624(2)(j) ("The court may provide, as further conditions of a sentence of 
probation" that the defendant "[u]ndergo available medical or 
mental health assessment and treatment[.]"). No similar analogue for the imposition of sex offender registration  exists 
under the sentencing statutes in HRS Chapter 706. Finally, the imposition of sex offender treatment  on an offender is not 
limited to a statutorily defined class of "covered offenders," 
as the sex offender registration  scheme in HRS Chapter 846E 
does. The State's argument lacks merit. 
3. The Circuit Court exceeded the scope of its
discretion in imposing sex offender
registration as a probation condition on anon-covered offender like Kauakahi. 
"In general, to constitute an abuse it must appear 
that the court clearly exceeded the bounds of reason or 
disregarded rules or principles of law or practice to the substantial detriment of a party litigant."   State v. Canosa, 
152 
Hawai ‘i 145, 154, 523 P.3d 1059, 1068 (2023). The exercise 
of discretion in this case did not comport with the applicable law of HRS § 846E-2, which excluded Kauakahi as a covered offender subject to the sex offender registration requirement. The Circuit Court's imposition of sex offender registration as a probation condition in this case thus disregarded the applicable law under HRS Chapter 846E, and exceeded the permissible scope

IV. CONCLUSION 
  
For the foregoing reasons, we reverse special 
condition "R" in the August 2, 2024 Probation Sentence, entered 
by the Circuit Court of the Fifth Circuit.
