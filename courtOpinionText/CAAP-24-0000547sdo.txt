(1) & (4) The Hawai #i Supreme Court recently re-
affirmed their case law regarding challenges to convictions based on allegedly defective charges. Relevant here, the supreme court

In a post-conviction challenge to a charge, the charge is
presumed valid and will be invalidated only if the defendantcan show (1) the charge cannot reasonably be construed toallege a crime; or (2) the defendant was prejudiced: Under the Motta /Wells rule, charges challenged for the first time
on appeal are presumed valid. Accordingly, we will onlyvacate a defendant's conviction under this standard if the defendant can show: (1) that the charge cannot reasonablybe construed to allege a crime; or (2) that the defendantwas prejudiced.

Here, because Smith challenges the sufficiency of the

applies. Therefore, the only bases for a challenge are that: (1) the charges cannot reasonably be construed to allege crimes; or (2) Smith was prejudiced.

With respect to Count 1, Smith argues neither, instead 
arguing that Count 1 of the Indictment failed to give notice of 
the correct state of mind for HRS § 707-756(1)(b). 
HRS § 707-756 includes three distinct elements with separate
mens rea requirements: (a) intentionally or knowinglycommunicating with a Minor; (b) agreeing to meet the Minorwith the intent to promote or facilitate the commission of afelony; and (c) intentionally or knowingly traveling to theagreed meeting place at the agreed time. 
State v. Alangcas, 134 Hawai #i 515, 525-26, 345 P.3d 181, 191-92 
(2015).

it failed to include the state of mind for Sexual Assault in the

First Degree, HRS § 707-730(1)(c). However, "it cannot logically 
be said that specifying the particular crime intended to be committed is, under our statutes, an essential element which must be alleged[.]" See State v. Robins , 66 Haw. 312, 315, 660 P.2d 
39, 41 (1983) (reversing dismissal of burglary indictment where prosecution did not specify underlying felony intent). Under HRS § 707-756(1)(b), "agreeing to meet the Minor with the intent to promote or facilitate the commission of a felony" does not require actual commission of a felony. See Alangcas
, 134 Hawai#i 
at 526, 345 P.3d at 192. In this case, the State alleged that Smith communicated with "Gillian" (Maui Police Department Detective Surina) with the intent to commit Sexual Assault in the First Degree. The elements of that second underlying offense, including the state of mind, need not be alleged. 
Smith's argument that Count 1 cannot reasonably be 
construed to state an offense is without merit. 
Smith makes no 
argument that he was prejudiced by alleged defects in Count 1. 
Smith also argues that Count 2 of the Indictment failed 
to allege a state of mind for the conduct "disseminate" and therefore cannot reasonably be construed to state an offense. HRS § 712-1215(1)(a) states that "[a] person commits the offense of promoting pornography for minors" if, "[k]nowing its character and content, the person disseminates to a minor material that is pornographic for minors[.]" Count 2 of the Indictment states that Smith "did disseminate to a minor material which is pornographic for minors, while knowing its character and content, specifically a digital picture of a penis[.]" The language of the Count 2 charge is almost verbatim with HRS § 712-1215(1)(a).

We conclude Count 2 can be reasonably construed as to allege a 
crime. The indirect reference to state of mind does not, on its own, render the charge defective. See State v. Moore , 82 Hawai #i 
202, 216, 921 P.2d 122, 136 (1996). Thus, we conclude that Count 2 of the Indictment of Smith was not deficient. 
Smith 
makes no argument that he was prejudiced by alleged defects in Count 2. 
Accordingly, Smith is not entitled to relief based on 
alleged defects in the Indictment. 
(3) Smith argues that the Circuit Court: erred by not 
conducting a Glenn colloquy; had a statutory duty to colloquy Smith pursuant to HRS § 704-407.5(1)-(2) (Supp. 2020); and by not doing so, failed to protect Smith's due process right to assert lack of penal responsibility by failing to make sure that his waiver of this defense was knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. 
The penal-responsibility defense stands in stark contrast
from all other affirmative defenses. While other defenses may negate a defendant's criminal liability, lack of penalresponsibility eliminates a defendant's moral culpability aswell. Moreover, unlike other affirmative defenses, adefendant who prevails on the penal-responsibility defensedoes not simply walk free – the determination ofirresponsibility becomes part of the defendant's record, andcan result in the defendant's commitment to an appropriateinstitution or supervision through conditional release.Given the significant consequences that can result, from apractical standpoint, a determination of lack of penalresponsibility is more akin to a guilty plea than anaffirmative defense. 
Glenn, 148 Hawai #i at 124, 468 P.3d at 138 (cleaned up). 
When a defendant seeks to waive the penal-
responsibility defense, the trial court must conduct an on-the-
record colloquy to confirm the "knowing, intelligent, and voluntary" waiver of that right. Id.  The Glenn Court adopted the New Jersey Supreme Court's approach in State v. Gorthy, 145 
6

A.3d 146, 157 (N.J. 2016), which outlined the following 
parameters: 
The court should explain to the defendant the nature and
purpose of the defense. It should generally describe theevidence relevant to that defense, including expert opinionthat could be used to support or counter that defense. The court should inform the defendant of his or her sentencingexposure in the event of a conviction. It should describe [] commitment and the other potential dispositions that areprescribed by [HRS § 704-411] in the event of an acquittalby reason of insanity. The court should confirm the defendant's understanding of the insanity defense as it mayaffect the outcome of the trial, defendant's risk ofincarceration and the prospect of civil commitment. 
Id. at 126, 468 P.3d at 140. 
If the waiver is knowing, intelligent, and voluntary, 
"the trial court must respect the decision of a competent 
defendant who is represented by counsel." Id.  
As with other fundamental rights, the right to assert 
lack of penal responsibility cannot be waived by counsel. It is solely the defendant's prerogative. Cf., e.g., State v. Vaitogi , 
59 Haw. 592, 601, 585 P.2d 1259, 1265 (1978) (guilty plea); State v. Ibuos, 75 
Hawai#i 118, 121, 857 P.2d 576, 578 (1993) (trial by 
jury); Tachibana v. State, 79 Hawai #i 226, 235–36, 900 P.2d 1293, 
1302–03 (1995) (right to testify); State v. Murray, 116 Hawai #i 
3, 21, 169 P.3d 955, 973 (2007) (right to have each element proven to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt); State v. Hernandez , 
143 Hawai #i 501, 515, 431 P.3d 1274, 1288 (2018) (no-contest 
plea); State v. Torres, 144 Hawai #i 282, 284-85, 439 P.3d 234, 
236-37 (2019) (right to remain silent); see also Treece v. State , 
547 A.2d 1054, 1062 (Md. 1988) ("The decision is one for the defendant to make, after proper consultation with counsel, just as a competent defendant must, ultimately, decide the wisdom of self-representation or of a plea of guilty.").

"[A]s with a Tachibana  colloquy, the [trial] court's 
only inquiry should be whether the defendant's decision to waive 
lack of penal responsibility as a defense is knowing, intelligent and voluntary – not whether it is wise ." Glenn, 148 Hawai #i at 
126, 468 P.3d at 140 (emphasis added). 
Thus, our inquiry focuses 
on whether the Circuit Court adequately advised Smith of his fundamental right to assert, or waive, this defense. 
The record on appeal shows at least two instances where 
the Circuit Court acknowledged Glenn . 
First, at trial, on the 
morning of April 16, 2024, outside the jury's presence, the State referred to the January 29, 2024 Notice of Defenses, saying, "there had been a defense of mental disease, disorder or defect filed." Further, "[t]he discussion with defense counsel this morning is she does not intend to rely upon it in a formal sense." The prosecutor voiced "concerns" over Glenn 's 
applicability, stating that Glenn  "said if a defendant who has a 
colorable defense under mental disorder or defect chooses not to invoke it, there's a colloquy to be done about the ramifications of those decisions." The State's position, prior to the discussion with defense counsel, was "[Smith] should also have a colloquy to know what the ramifications are if he was found acquitted by version of mental disorder or defect, which is commitment to State Hospital for the rest of his life, being found dangerous, registration as a sex offender. All those kind of things." The prosecutor explained, "I think there are questions raised about how you have to colloquy the defendant or advise him of those ramifications." "The due process guarantee of the Hawai #i Constitution 
serves to protect the right of an accused in a criminal case to a fundamentally fair trial, and central to the protections of due process is the right to be accorded a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense." 
Glenn, 148 Hawai #i at 119-20, 468 
P.3d at 133-34 (citation omitted). We conclude that the Circuit Court erred when it failed to colloquy Smith as Glenn requires. 
Accordingly, on this basis, we vacate Smith's 
conviction. As our resolution of Smith's third point of error is dispositive, we decline to reach Smith's further arguments.
