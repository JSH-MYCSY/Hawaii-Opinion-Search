B. Procedural Background 
Tualaulelei failed to appear in court in both cases on 
November 13, 2024. As a result, the circuit court issued a bench warrant for Tualaulelei's arrest. The next day, on 
November 14, 2024, the circuit court entered its "Judgment and 
Order of Forfeiture of Bail Bond" (Forfeiture Judgment) in both 
cases. (Formatting altered.) 
The State filed a proof of service of the Forfeiture 
Judgment upon Universal with the circuit court, indicating service was by certified mail. The proof of service showed the 
postmark date as November 18, 2024, and the receipt date as 
November 25, 2024. 
Tualaulelei was arrested on December 5, 2024, and a 
Return of Service for the bench warrant was filed the next day. 
Thirty-three days after the receipt date, on 
December 28, 2024, Shaka moved to set aside the Forfeiture 
Judgment (Motion to Set Aside or Motion).  The circuit court denied Shaka's Motion as untimely. 
Shaka appealed, and we consolidated the two cases.

We begin with Shaka's argument that good cause existed 
to set aside the Forfeiture Judgment, because Tualaulelei was

A. No Abuse of Discretion in Denying Untimely Motion to Set 
Aside Forfeiture Judgment 
Shaka argues that good cause existed to set aside the 
Forfeiture Judgment. In particular, Shaka appears to argue that 
good cause existed because Tualaulelei was returned to custody 
before the thirty-day time period to set aside the Forfeiture 
Judgment expired. Shaka posits that "[r]igidly applying procedural formalities to deny such relief frustrates the 
statute's equitable purpose." 
By failing to ensure the defendant's appearance, a 
bond is forfeited. State v. Nelson, 140 Hawaiʻ i 123, 132 n.13, 
398 P.3d 712, 721 n.13 (2017). Under 
Hawaiʻi Revised Statutes 
(HRS) § 804-51 (Supp. 2023), when a bond is forfeited, the court 
must immediately enter judgment and execute that judgment thirty 
days after giving notice of the entry of judgment, unless a 
motion showing good cause is filed within those thirty days: 
§804-51 Procedure. Whenever the court, in any 
criminal cause, forfeits any bond or recognizance given in a 
criminal cause, the court shall immediately enter up 
judgment in favor of the State and against the principal or 
principals, surety or sureties, and surety insurer or surety insurers on the bond, jointly and severally, for the full 
amount of the penalty thereof, and shall cause execution to 
issue thereon immediately after the expiration of thirty 
days from the date that notice is given via personal service

  or certified mail, return receipt requested, to the surety 
or sureties or the surety insurer or surety insurers on the 
bond, of the entry of the judgment in favor of the State, unless before the expiration of thirty days from the date 
that notice is given to the surety or sureties or the surety insurer or surety insurers on the bond of the entry of the 
judgment in favor of the State, a motion or application of 
the principal or principals, surety or sureties, surety 
insurer or surety insurers, or any of them, showing good 
cause why execution should not issue upon the judgment, is filed with the court. 
(Emphases added.) 
In other words, a surety either guarantees a 
defendant's appearance in court or it forfeits the bond. And 
should the bond be forfeited, a forfeiture judgment is entered 
and the surety is given an opportunity to stay execution of the 
forfeiture judgment through the "unless" clause. 
The "unless" clause provides a conditional exception 
to the mandatory and immediate execution of the forfeiture 
judgment at the expiration of thirty days. The condition 
requires, "before the expiration of thirty days from" receiving notice of the judgment, a "motion or application" "showing good 
cause" to be "filed with the court." State v. Vaimili, 131 
Hawaiʻi 9, 15, 313 P.3d 698, 704 (2013) ("[O]nce a bond is 
forfeited pursuant to HRS § 804-51, a surety has thirty days 
from the time it receives notice of forfeiture to set aside the 
forfeiture judgment[.]"). 
HRS § 804-51 provides no further remedy to stay 
execution of a forfeiture judgment once the thirty days in the 
"unless" clause expires. State v. Deplae
, 158 Hawaiʻi 225, 590

 P.3d 354, No. CAAP-24-0000492, 2026 WL 1683929, at *2 (App. 
June 10, 2026) (SDO) ("A circuit court is 'without power to 
consider' a motion to set aside a forfeiture that is 'not filed 
within the time limit imposed by HRS § 804-51[.]'") (citation omitted). 
Here, Shaka guaranteed Tualaulelei would appear at 
court or it would forfeit the bond. 
Shaka did not honor its 
guarantee. Because Tualaulelei did not appear in court, the circuit court entered the Forfeiture Judgment. 
Substantial evidence in the record supports the 
challenged finding that notice of the Forfeiture Judgment was 
received on November 25, 2024, as the State provided a copy of 
the signed and dated return receipt. 
Any motion to set aside the Forfeiture Judgment was to 
be filed "before the expiration of thirty days" — here, December 25, 2024. Because December 25 was a holiday, Shaka's 
Motion must have been "filed with the court" by December 26, 
2024. HRS §§ 1-29, -32 (2009).  Shaka, however, filed its 
Motion on December 28, 2024, two days after the deadline. 
Shaka's Motion did not comply with time requirements set forth in HRS § 804-51. 
Shaka nonetheless argues that, because Tualaulelei was 
in custody before the thirty days expired, good cause existed 
for staying execution of the Forfeiture Judgment.

 In State v. Camara, the Hawaiʻ i Supreme Court 
reiterated that a forfeiture judgment shall be executed upon the  
expiration of thirty days, "unless  
the principal or the surety 
(or both) files a motion or application 'showing good cause why 
execution should not issue upon the judgment,' HRS § 804-51,  
prior to the expiration of the thirty-day search period." 81 
Hawaiʻi 324, 330, 916 P.2d 1225, 1231 (1996) (second emphasis  
added).  
Furthermore, "'good cause why execution should not 
issue upon the judgment' of forfeiture may be satisfied by the 
defendant, prior to the expiration of the thirty-day search 
period: (1) providing a satisfactory reason for his or her 
failure to appear when required; or (2) surrendering or being 
surrendered." Id. 
at 330, 916 P.2d at 1231. "Surrendering" 
means "both voluntary and involuntary surrender by the principal to law enforcement officials[.]" Id. at 332, 916 P.2d at 1233. 
The supreme court explained that "returning the bond 
to the surety, less costs, is consistent with the general 
principle that the primary purpose of bail is not to punish the defendant or surety, . . . but to secure the presence of the 
defendant to answer the charges against him or her." Id. at 
332, 916 P.2d at 1233. Accordingly, "if the State regains custody of the defendant prior to the expiration of the thirty-
day search period and the surety receives partial return of its 
7

  bond, the primary purpose of bail would be met. To hold 
otherwise would result in punishment of the surety and possibly 
of the defendant." Id. 
On December 6, 2024, the Return of Service was filed 
with the court, which was before the expiration of thirty days. And that Return of Service showed Tualaulelei was arrested on 
December 5, 2024, which was also before the expiration of thirty 
days. 
Based on Camara, the Return of Service could have 
provided a basis for good cause in a motion or application as it showed Tualaulelei was in custody, i.e., surrendered before the 
expiration of the thirty days. 
However, Shaka waited twenty-three days after 
Tualaulelei was arrested, and twenty-two days after the Return of Service was filed, to move to set aside the Forfeiture 
Judgment, resulting in the Motion being two days late. In other 
words, Shaka simply sat on its rights. 
At some point, there must be finality in a judgment. 
In re Peoro, 793 F.2d 1048, 1052 (9th Cir. 1986) ("One of the 
fundamental requirements of a judicial system is finality."). 
Here, the legislature set the point of finality by plainly stating that the judgment shall be executed "immediately after 
the expiration of thirty days." HRS § 804-51.

 It is not for this court to expand this period of 
time, and we leave it to the legislature to amend HRS § 804-51 
if it did not intend this result. 
Consistent with Camara, which reiterated that a motion 
or application showing good cause must be made "prior to the expiration of the thirty-day search period," we hold that the 
circuit court did not abuse its discretion by denying Shaka's 
Motion. See 81 Hawai
ʻi at 330, 916 P.2d at 1231; U.S. v. Amwest 
Sur. Ins. Co., 54 F.3d 601, 602 (9th Cir. 1995) ("We review a 
district court's decision to set aside or remit the forfeiture 
of an appearance bond for abuse of discretion."). 
B. Shaka's Remaining Arguments Are Waived or Without Merit 
1. Service 
Shaka contends service was improper.  In its opening 
brief, Shaka acknowledges that Darci Todd was Universal's 
secretary and that Todd signed the return receipt on 
November 25, 2024, but argues that it "never agreed that . . . 
Todd was its agent[.]" Shaka then argues that, under HRS 
§ 431:2-205 (2019), the State failed to serve notice upon the 
insurance commissioner. 
Shaka, however, fails to point to where in the record 
it raised these arguments before the circuit court.  Shaka 
argued, both in its Motion to Set Aside and at the hearing, that 
its Motion was timely because it had until December 30, 2024 to

and that good cause existed. 
Shaka made no argument regarding Todd or HRS § 431:2-
205, and we need not consider unpresented arguments. See HRS 
§ 641-2 (2016) ("The appellate court . . . need not consider a 
point that was not presented in the trial court in an

Hawai

if a party does not raise an argument at the circuit court 
level, that argument will be deemed to have been waived on appeal[.]" (citation modified)). 
Thus, we deem these arguments waived. 2. HRCP Does Not Apply 
Shaka next contends the circuit court should have 
applied the rules of civil procedure to afford HRCP Rule 60(b) 
relief. But HRCP Rule 81(a)(8) provides that the HRCP does not 
apply to "[p]roceedings for the forfeiture of bonds." Vaimili, 
131 Hawaiʻ i at 14, 313 P.3d at 703 (reiterating that "HRCP 
Rule 81(a) provides that the rules of civil procedure do not 
apply to bond forfeiture proceedings.").  Thus, the HRCP does 
not apply in the instant case. 
Shaka then argues that the circuit court should have 
applied the HRCP by invoking HRCP Rule 81(h), which provides, "[i]n any proceeding in the land court or listed in subdivision

But Shaka does not point to where in the record it

3. Due Process 
Finally, Shaka contends it was denied due process. 
Shaka argues the statute is overbroad and the circuit court's "interpretation of HRS § 804-51 offers no mechanism for judicial consideration of good faith, mistake, or the actual recovery of 
the defendant." Shaka argues "the statute fails rational basis 
review." 
Shaka fails to show where in the record it raised this 
argument before the circuit court. See State v. Biton
