 On appeal, Pruin contends the Rule 40 Court erred by 
denying her proposed findings, conclusions, and order, and by 
dismissing her Hawaiʻi Rules of Penal Procedure (HRPP) Rule 40 
petition (Rule 40 Petition) without a hearing.  To support her 
contentions, Pruin alleges the trial court did not advise her of 
the immigration consequences of her no contest plea.  She thus 
claims her no contest plea was not entered into voluntarily and 
knowingly. 
Upon careful review of the record and the briefs 
submitted by the parties, and having given due consideration to 
the issues raised and the arguments advanced, we resolve this appeal as discussed below and affirm.   
In 2003 and 2004, Respondent-Appellee the State of 
Hawaiʻi charged Pruin with various drug offenses arising from 
three separate incidents in case numbers 2PC031000290, 2PC031000621, and 2PC041000054 (the underlying criminal cases). 
In March 2004, the parties stipulated to, and the 
trial court granted, a continuance of the pretrial motions 
"based upon the need for the Defendant to explore immigration 
issues and for both parties to continue to work on a possible 
plea agreement."  In June 2004, the trial court granted a second 
continuance to allow Pruin additional time "to explore immigration issues" and the parties further opportunity to 
negotiate a possible plea agreement.

 We review the denial of an HRPP Rule 40 petition 
without a hearing de novo.  Dan v. State, 76 Hawaiʻ i 423, 427, 
879 P.2d 528, 532 (1994). 
"The U.S. Constitution and the Hawaiʻ i Constitution 
require a plea to be voluntarily and knowingly entered."  Eason 
v. State, 157 Hawaiʻ i 252, 271, 576 P.3d 765, 784 (2025).  
"Hawai
ʻi case law requires the circuit court [to] make an 
affirmative showing by an on-the-record colloquy wherein the 
defendant is shown to have a full understanding of what the plea 
of guilty . . . connotes and its consequences."  Id. at 272, 576 
P.3d at 785 (citation modified). 
"When the record is not silent or minimal, the 
petitioner seeking relief under Rule 40 on the ground that the guilty plea was entered involuntarily has the burden 'to prove 
by a preponderance of the evidence that his constitutional right 
was not voluntarily and intelligently waived.'"  Id. (quoting 
Eli v. State, 63 Haw. 474, 477, 630 P.2d 113, 116 (1981)). 
HRPP Rule 40(f) dictates whether an evidentiary 
hearing is required: 
If a petition alleges facts that if proven would entitle 
the petitioner to relief, the court shall grant a hearing which may extend only to the issues raised in the petition 
or answer.  However, the court may deny a hearing if the 
petitioner's claim is patently frivolous and is without 
trace of support either in the record or from other 
evidence submitted by the petitioner.

 Thus, a hearing should be held if the HRPP Rule 40 petition 
states a colorable claim.  "To establish a colorable claim, the 
allegations of the petition must show that if taken as true the 
facts alleged would change the verdict."  Dan, 76 Hawaiʻi at 427, 
879 P.2d at 532 (quoting State v. Allen, 7 Haw. App. 89, 92, 744 
P.2d 789, 792 (App. 1987)). 
But the court need not hold a hearing if the claim is 
patently frivolous and without a trace of support either in the 
record or from other evidence the petitioner submits.  HRPP 
Rule 40(f).  It is not error to deny the HRPP Rule 40 petition 
without a hearing "[w]here examination of the record of the trial court proceedings indicates that the petitioner's 
allegations show no colorable claim."  Dan, 76 
Hawaiʻi at 427, 
879 P.2d at 532 (quoting Allen, 7 Haw. App. at 92, 744 P.2d at 
792). 
We note that, at the time of Pruin's change of plea 
hearing in 2004, HRS § 802E-2 required a different immigration advisement than it does today.  HRS § 802E-2 (1993) provided: 
Court advisement concerning alien status required.  
Prior to acceptance of a plea of guilty or nolo contendere 
to any offense punishable as a crime under state law, 
except offenses designated as infractions under state law, 
the court shall administer the following advisement on the 
record to the defendant: 
 If you are not a citizen of the United States, you 
are hereby advised that conviction of the offense for 
which you have been charged may have the consequences of deportation, exclusion from admission to the 
United States, or denial of naturalization pursuant 
to the laws of the United States.

We also note that, prior to the change of plea 
hearing, Pruin twice stipulated to continuances of the 
underlying criminal cases to allow time "to explore immigration 
issues and . . . to work on a possible plea agreement," 
demonstrating that Pruin was aware of potential immigration consequences.  And Pruin's change of plea form contained several 
advisements, including one that substantially tracked the 
original advisement of HRS § 802E-2 and another that informed 
Pruin the State would urge HPA to release her for deportation.   
As such, Pruin's claim that her no contest plea was 
not made knowingly and voluntarily (based on her allegation that 
the trial court failed to advise her of the immigration 
consequences) is patently frivolous and without a trace of 
support in the record.  See HRPP Rule 40(f); see also Den Van 
Nguyen v. State, 128 Hawaiʻi 311, 288 P.3d 129, No. CAAP-11-
0000575, 2012 WL 4454735, at *2 (App. Sep. 26, 2012) (SDO) 
(holding petitioner "waived his right to seek relief based on 
HRS §§ 802E-2 and 802E-3," because he "waited until after the NOT FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAIʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER
