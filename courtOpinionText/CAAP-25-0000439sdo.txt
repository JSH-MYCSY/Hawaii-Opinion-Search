II. Discussion 
The Family Court entered the OFP pursuant to HRS 
§ 586-5.5. That statute provides in relevant part: 
Protective order; additional orders.  (a) If, after
hearing all relevant evidence, the court finds that the
respondent has failed to show cause why the [temporaryrestraining] order should not be continued and that aprotective order is necessary to prevent domestic abuse or arecurrence of abuse, the court may order that a protectiveorder be issued for a further fixed reasonable period as thecourt deems appropriate . . . . 
HRS § 586-5.5(a) (Supp. 2021) (emphasis added). At a protective 
order hearing under HRS § 586-5.5(a), "the burden remains on the petitioner to prove the petitioner's underlying allegations by a preponderance of the evidence." Kie v. McMahel, 91 Hawai# i 438, 
442, 984 P.2d 1264, 1268 (App. 1999) (citing Coyle v. Compton, 85 Hawai#i 197, 205, 940 P.2d 404, 412 (App. 1997)). 
HRS § 586-1 (Supp. 2020) defines "[d]omestic abuse" in 
part as "[p]hysical harm, bodily injury, assault, or the threat of imminent physical harm, bodily injury, or assault, extreme psychological abuse, coercive control, or malicious property damage between family or household members[.]" (Emphasis added.)

In turn, HRS § 586-1 (2018) defines "[e]xtreme psychological 
abuse" as "an intentional or knowing course of conduct directed at an individual that seriously alarms or disturbs consistently or continually bothers the individual, and that serves no legitimate purpose; provided that such course of conduct would cause a reasonable person to suffer extreme emotional distress." 
Here, based on the FOFs, the Family Court concluded in 
relevant part: 
28. [Cheng] met her burden to establish that [Stoler]
engaged in an intentional or knowing course of conduct
directed at [Cheng] that seriously alarmed or disturbed[Cheng], serving no legitimate purpose. 
29. The multiple actions of [Stoler] in their
totality, before and after his breakup with [Cheng],constituted a course of conduct that would cause a reasonable person to suffer extreme emotional distress. 
30. [Cheng] met her burden to establish that [Stoler]
subjected her to extreme psychological abuse as defined inHRS § 586-1. 
31. [Cheng] met her burden to establish that [Stoler]
committed domestic abuse. 
. . . . 
33. A Protective Order is necessary to prevent
domestic abuse or the reoccurrence of abuse. 
Stoler contends that the Family Court erred in reaching 
COLs 28-31. Specifically, he challenges COL 28 in his first 
point of error, COL 29 in his second point of error, and COLs 30-31 in his third and sixth points of error. See supra. 
We review the Family Court's exercise of discretion in 
this context as follows: 
[T]he family court possesses wide discretion in making its
decisions and those decision[s] will not be set aside unlessthere is a manifest abuse of discretion. Thus, we will notdisturb the family court's decisions on appeal unless thefamily court disregarded rules or principles of law orpractice to the substantial detriment of a party litigantand its decision clearly exceeded the bounds of reason. 
Fisher v. Fisher, 111 Hawai# i 41, 46, 137 P.3d 355, 360 (2006) 
(quoting In re Doe, 95 
Hawai#i 183, 189-90, 20 P.3d 616, 622-23 
(2001)).

We review the Family Court's findings of fact for clear 
error and vacate only when "the record lacks substantial evidence 
to support the finding or, . . . despite substantial evidence in support of the finding, [we are] left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made." Id."'Substantial 
evidence' is credible evidence which is of sufficient quality and probative value to enable a person of reasonable caution to support a conclusion." Id.We likewise review conclusions of 
law that present mixed questions of fact and law for clear error. See Estate of Klink ex rel. Klink v. State, 113 Hawai# i 332, 351, 
152 P.3d 504, 523 (2007) (quoting AIG Hawaii Ins. Co. v. Estate of Caraang, 74 Haw. 620, 629, 851 P.2d 321, 326 (1993)). 
Here, the unchallenged FOFs, summarized above, are 
binding on this court and support the Family Court's mixed conclusions of fact and law in COLs 28 through 31. See In re 
Doe, 99 Hawai# i at 538, 57 P.3d at 463. These COLs are supported 
by substantial evidence in the record, and we are not left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made. See 
In re Doe, 95 Hawai# i at 190, 20 P.3d at 623. 
Stoler argues that he "never intended nor knowingly 
engaged in a course of conduct to seriously alarm, or disturb" Cheng; his "mimicking of 'Love Lines' . . . [was] motivated out of friendship and/or love between the parties"; and "[a]ttempting to repair a basic friendship or even a romantic relationship necessarily infers an intent that could not be one to harass, annoy, or alarm. 
Because "intent can rarely be proved by direct 
evidence, proof of circumstantial evidence and reasonable inferences arising from circumstances surrounding the act is sufficient to establish the requisite intent." See State v. 
Calaycay, 145 Hawai# i 186, 200, 449 P.3d 1184, 1198 (2019) 
(quoting State v. Kiese, 126 Hawai# i 494, 502, 273 P.3d 1180, 
1188 (2012)). 
Here, Cheng's testimony and the documentary 
evidence, as reflected in the unchallenged FOFs, constituted substantial evidence from which the Family Court could reasonably have concluded that Stoler engaged in an intentional or knowing course of conduct directed at Cheng that seriously alarmed or

disturbed her, serving no legitimate purpose. In disputing his 
intent, Stoler argues the weight of the evidence, ignoring the Family Court's express findings that Cheng's testimony was "credible in full" and, in several relevant instances, Stoler's testimony was not credible. We will not pass on the credibility of a witness or the weight of the evidence. See Fisher, 111 
Hawai#i at 46, 137 P.3d at 360 (quoting In re Doe, 95 Hawai# i at 
190, 20 P.3d at 623). Substantial evidence supports COL 28. 
Stoler argues that the Family Court erred in concluding 
that the totality of his actions constituted a course of conduct that would cause a reasonable person to suffer extreme emotional distress. In particular, he contends that his 23 emails to Cheng on August 27, 2024, were "[l]egitimate communicated efforts on the part of a person in love [that] can hardly be conduct that would cause a reasonable person to suffer extreme emotional distress. 
We disagree. The unchallenged FOFs support the Family 
Court's conclusion that the totality of Stoler's actions constituted a course of conduct that would cause a reasonable person to suffer extreme emotional distress, as reflected in COL 29. In this regard, Stoler's fifth point of error (see supra) also lacks merit. 
The Family Court did not conclude that the 23 
emails in isolation were "stalking and abnormal behavior, and domestic abuse." Rather, it was Stoler who testified that the emails were not such behavior. Substantial evidence supports COL 29. 
Finally, Stoler contends in his fourth point of error 
that the Family Court erred in finding that the Petition "listed 3 specific allegations of abuse, and dates for the specific acts, and unspecified acts." He makes no argument supporting this contention or its significance. It is deemed waived.
