We note that Vicente's opening brief does not comply

To promote access to justice, we liberally interpret a 
self-represented litigant's briefs and do not automatically 
foreclose them from appellate review because they fail to comply 
with court rules.  Erum v. Llego, 147 Hawaiʻi 368, 380-81, 465 
P.3d 815, 827-28 (2020).  We thus address Vicente's arguments to 
the extent we can discern. 
We first address the challenges to our jurisdiction 
over this case.  Although he appealed to this court from the Dismissal Order, Vicente nonetheless appears to challenge this 
court's jurisdiction based on the illegal overthrow of the 
Hawaiian Kingdom.  The Hawaiʻ i Supreme Court has reaffirmed that 
"whatever may be said regarding the lawfulness of its origins, the State of 
Hawaiʻi is now, a lawful government."  State v. 
Kaulia, 128 Hawaiʻ i 479, 487, 291 P.3d 377, 385 (2013) (citation

 Defendant-Appellee Kamaʻ aina Nissan Inc. argues this 
court lacks jurisdiction over this appeal as there was no final 
judgment or order.  Following briefing, we remanded this case to the district court for entry of a written order.  On October 22, 
2026, the district court entered the Dismissal Order and 
dismissed Vicente's claims without prejudice.  See Price v. 
Obayashi Hawaii Corp., 81 Hawaiʻ i 171, 175, 914 P.2d 1364, 1368 
(1996) (reiterating that a "[d]ismissal without prejudice does 
not affect appellate jurisdiction" (formatting altered)).  Thus, 
we have jurisdiction over this matter. 
To the extent Vicente appeals from the Dismissal 
Order, Vicente argues the district court "violated my Due 
Process Rights by not letting me amend my complaint" and "did 
not give me a legal reason." 
"The basic elements of procedural due process of law 
require notice and an opportunity to be heard at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner."  Korean Buddhist Dae Won Sa 
Temple of Hawaii v. Sullivan, 87 
Hawaiʻi 217, 243, 953 P.2d 1315, 
1341 (1998) (citation modified). 
Vicente filed a "Complaint (Assumpsit-Money Owed)" on 
April 8, 2025.  The form complaint alleged Nissan owed him 
$6,891.14.  Vicente did not attach any written instrument on 
which the amount claimed was based. NOT FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAIʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER

 According to the court minutes, the district court 
held a hearing on April 30, 2025 (April 30, 2025 Hearing).  
Nissan acknowledged receipt of the complaint.  The district 
court expressed concerns regarding the complaint and ordered 
Vicente "to file supplemental to complaint as to what is being 
asked for and why."  The district court continued the matter and 
set a further hearing. 
About an hour after the April 30, 2025 Hearing, 
Vicente filed with the court a September 3, 2024 letter from the 
State of Hawaiʻi Regulated Industries Complaints Office to Nissan 
regarding a complaint Vicente registered, and Nissan's 
September 12, 2024 response (the Letters). 
At the further hearing held on June 4, 2025, the 
district court first noted that it was in receipt of the Letters.  The district court then noted that Vicente "wishes to 
amend complaint and request more money/make additional claims."  
  
The district court dismissed the matter without prejudice, allowed Vicente to refile, and waived the filing fees. 
In sum, the district court provided Vicente an 
opportunity to supplement his complaint.  The Letters, however, did not show the existence of a written instrument on which 
Vicente's claim that Nissan owed him money was based.  The 
minutes then show that Vicente wanted to amend his complaint for 
more money and to add more claims, and the district court NOT FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAIʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
 
5 
 dismissed the matter without prejudice to Vicente refiling.  But 
"[w]ithout the relevant transcript, there is insufficient 
evidence to review the alleged error, and [the appellant] 
carries the burden of demonstrating the alleged error in the 
record."  State v. Hoang, 93 Hawaiʻ i 333, 336, 3 P.3d 499, 502 
(2000). 
Moreover, Vicente's principal argument is that the 
district court violated his due process rights by not allowing 
him to amend his complaint, when in fact it appears that the 
district court in effect afforded him the opportunity to amend 
by dismissing without prejudice and allowing Vicente to refile 
without the payment of a further filing fee.  Accordingly, we 
cannot say the district court violated Vicente's due process 
rights. 
Based on the foregoing, we affirm the district court's 
October 22, 2026 Dismissal Order.
