(1) Husband's first point of error is that the Family 
Court erred when it awarded Wife the Marital Home. He contends that the Family Court deviated from the partnership model without adequately identifying equitable considerations. This argument is without merit. 
HRS § 580-47(a) (Supp. 2025) authorizes the Family 
Court, after granting divorce, to enter orders that are "just and equitable" in finally dividing and distributing the parties' estate. In doing so, the Family Court must consider, among other things, "the relative abilities of the parties, the condition in which each party will be left by the divorce, the burdens imposed upon either party for the benefit of the children . . . and all other circumstances." HRS § 580-47(a).  The Family Court has wide discretion in dividing marital partnership property, and its ultimate division is reviewed for 
abuse of discretion. See Kakinami v. Kakinami, 127 Hawaiʻ i 126, 
136, 276 P.3d 695, 705 (2012) ("[W]e will not disturb the family 
court's decision on appeal unless the family court disregarded 
rules or principles of law or practice to the substantial detriment of a party litigant and its decision clearly exceeded 
the bounds of reason." (quoting Fisher v. Fisher, 111 Hawaiʻ i 41, 
46, 137 P.3d 355, 360 (2006)) (block quote formatting omitted)). Under Hawaiʻ i's P
artnership Model: 
the family court [shall] first find all of the facts 
necessary for categorization of the properties and 
assignment of the relevant net market values. Second, the 
court must identify any equitable considerations justifying 
deviation from an equal distribution. Third, the court 
must decide whether or not there will be a deviation, and in its fourth step, the court decides the extent of any 
deviation. 
Gordon v. Gordon, 135 Hawaiʻ i 340, 350, 350 P.3d 1008, 1018 
(2015) (cleaned up). 
"[W]hether facts present valid and

 relevant considerations authorizing a deviation from the 
partnership model division is a question of law that this court reviews under the right/wrong standard of appellate review." Id. at 348, 350 P.3d at 1016. 
Relying on Hamilton v. Hamilton, 138 Hawaiʻ i 185, 204-
205, 378 P.3d 901, 920-921 (2016), Husband argues the Family 
Court was required to identify the equitable considerations 
justifying deviation from an equal distribution and that its purported "after-the-fact justification" was insufficient.  Here, the Family Court identified valid and relevant considerations supporting its decision to award Wife the Marital Home. The Decision and Order found Wife's testimony credible. The Family Court further found that the Condo was not suitable for Wife and the Children because of one child's disabilities.  In its FOFs, the Family Court likewise found that the Marital Home was "better suited . . . to secure and protect" the child. The Family Court also considered Wife's future financial circumstances, noting that the Marital Home has no mortgage and no electricity bill due to the solar panels on the roof. These 
considerations directly relate to Wife's post-divorce 
circumstances and the burdens associated with caring for the child with disabilities, both of which HRS § 580-47(a) require the Family Court to consider. 
Here, unlike in Hamilton, the Family Court identified 
the equitable considerations supporting deviation when it distributed the parties' property. See id. Accordingly, the 
Family Court did not abuse its discretion in awarding Wife the Marital Home. 
(2) Husband's second point of error is that the Family 
Court abused its discretion in failing to account for his child-support obligation when determining his ability to pay spousal support. He argues that the Family Court "ignored [Husband's]

 ability to meet his own projected needs over three years after 
the divorce." Relying on the Family Court's later interim child support award of $2,656.00, Husband argues that the correct calculation for his monthly surplus is $2,953.95 rather than $5,609.95. 
HRS § 580-47(a) authorizes the Family Court to award 
spousal support as "just and equitable" and requires 
consideration of, among other factors, the parties' financial 
resources and needs, custodial and child-support responsibilities, and the supporting spouse's ability to meet his or her own needs while providing support. "There is no fixed rule for determining the amount of property to be awarded each spouse in a divorce action other than as set forth in HRS § 580-47."  Au-Hoy v. Au-Hoy, 60 Haw. 354, 357, 590 P.2d 80, 82 (1979). 
The record supports the Family Court's determination 
that Wife is entitled to spousal support based on the parties' twenty-eight-year marriage, Wife's reduced earning capacity and caregiving responsibilities, the parties' respective financial circumstances, Wife's lifetime continuing responsibility and 
care for the child with disabilities, and Wife's debt incurred 
due to Husband's failure to provide financial support during the divorce proceedings. Nevertheless, it appears that the Family Court did not expressly consider Husband's child support obligations in ordering spousal support. See HRS § 580-47(a) 
("[T]he court, in ordering spousal support and maintenance, shall consider the following factors: . . . (10) Custodial and child support responsibilities; (11) Ability of the party from whom support and maintenance is sought to meet the party's own needs while meeting the needs of the party seeking support and maintenance[.]" (formatting altered)). We therefore vacate the award of spousal support and remand for the Family Court's

 reconsideration of this award in accordance with HRS § 580-47.  
To be clear, we do not  hold that spousal support must be reduced 
by $21,996.00 ($611.00 x 36 months), $2,656.00 per month, or any other amount; we hold only that the Family Court must specifically consider Husband's child support obligation under HRS §§ 580-47(a)(10) and (a)(11) in calculating his spousal  
support obligation. See Jacoby v. Jacoby  , 150 Hawaiʻ i 158, 166, 
498 P.3d 689, 697 (2021).  
The Family Court awarded the Lot to Wife to offset 
spousal support owed by Husband. Because we vacate the award of spousal support, we also vacate the Family Court's award of the Lot to Wife. As such, we need not reach Husband's third point of error. 
Accordingly, the spousal support award is vacated and 
remanded for reconsideration of Husband's child support obligation pursuant to HRS §§ 580-47(a)(10) and (a)(11).  We also vacate the Lot transfer from the July 15, 2025 Decision and Order and Divorce Decree because it was used to satisfy part of Husband's spousal support obligation. 
For the foregoing reasons, we affirm in part and 
vacate in part the Family Court of the First Circuit's July 15, 2025 Divorce Decree and Decision and Order and remand for further proceedings consistent with this Summary Disposition Order.
