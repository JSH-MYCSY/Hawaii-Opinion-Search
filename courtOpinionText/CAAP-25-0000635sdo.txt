Upon careful review of the record and the briefs 
submitted by the parties, and having given due consideration to 
the arguments advanced and the issues raised, we resolve Mother's arguments below. In short, we affirm the Family Court's orders as to points 1, 2, 5, and 6 listed above. We 
vacate the September 9, 2025 Order regarding 3 (denial of 
Mother's Motion for Financial Evaluation) and 4 (termination of child support) in part, as explained more fully below. 
(1) Mother challenges the Family Court's Order 
Vacating Grant of Reconsideration.  However, we lack jurisdiction over this order. This order — vacating a previous order granting a motion for reconsideration — was a final order: it left nothing to be decided, and was thus appealable as a 
post-decree order.  See Ditto v. McCurdy, 103 Hawaiʻ i 153, 157, 
80 P.3d 974, 978 (2003) ("A post-judgment order is an appealable final order under [Hawai
ʻi Revised Statutes (HRS)] § 641–1(a) if 
the order ends the proceedings, leaving nothing further to be 
7           NOT FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAIʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
 
 
 
  
 
 
 
 
 
  
 
  
 
 
 
 
  
 accomplished. Correlatively, an order is not final if the 
rights of a party involved remain undetermined or if the matter is retained for further action." (internal citation omitted)). Mother did not challenge this order until her September 17, 2025 appeal.  Her challenge is therefore untimely. See HRAP Rule 
4(a)(1) ("When a civil appeal is permitted by law, the notice of appeal shall be filed within 30 days after entry of the judgment 
or appealable order."). 
(2)(a-b) Mother next challenges the Second Parent 
Coordinator Order. We have jurisdiction over this challenge 
because the Second Parent Coordinator Order was not "final" until the Family Court denied the request to find Mother in contempt (thus finally resolving all issues) in the September 9, 2025 Order, and because Mother's appeal from that Order was timely. 
Mother contends that the Second Parent Coordinator 
Order is erroneous because the Family Court had no authority to appoint a new Parent Coordinator without the parties' agreement. She further argues the Family Court lacked evidence to support the appointment of a Parent Coordinator. These arguments are 
without merit. The Divorce Decree provided as follows: 
The parties shall mutually agree to the appointment 
of two (2) parent coordinators ("P.C."), one in California 
and one in Hawaii, the costs to be shared equally by the 
parties. The P.C. in California shall address all issues 
that arise when [Child] is in California. The P.C. in Hawaii shall address all issues that arise when [Child] is 
in Hawaii. The P.C.s may communicate with each other, as 
necessary. In the event the parties are unable to agree 
who the P.C.'s shall be, each party shall submit at least 
one name to the Court, who shall make the decision. 
The parties subsequently stipulated to the use of a single 
Parent Coordinator — Dr. Simon — but that "[a]ll terms of the Divorce Decree not inconsistent herewith shall remain in full force and effect." In other words, Mother stipulated to the use 
8
           NOT FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAIʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
 
 
 
  
 
 
  
 
  
 
  
  
 
 
 
 
  
 of a single Parent Coordinator and further stipulated that the 
remaining provisions of the Divorce Decree — which included a process for appointment of a Parent Coordinator if the parties could not agree — would remain in effect. Thus, the legal authority for the Family Court's appointment of the Parent Coordinator is the Divorce Decree itself, as carried forward by the parties' stipulation. Mother makes no argument that the 
Family Court or Father failed to follow this process. 
Accordingly, her argument that the Family Court's appointment of the Parent Coordinator was improper is without merit. 
(2)(c) Mother challenges the Family Court's award of 
attorney's fees. However, the Fees and Costs Order was a final order — with nothing left to be decided — and Mother did not file a timely appeal from that order. As such, we lack 
jurisdiction to consider it. See Ditto, 103 Hawaiʻ i at 157, 80 
P.3d at 978. Similarly, Mother's Motion for Financial 
Evaluation (filed on June 26, 2025) requested the Family Court to set aside the award of attorney's fees. This portion of Mother's Motion for Financial Evaluation, however, was simply an 
untimely motion for reconsideration under Rule 59(e) of the 
Hawai
ʻi Family Court Rules.  We lack jurisdiction to consider it. 
See HRAP Rule 4(a)(3) (requiring a "timely" motion for 
reconsideration to extend the time to file a notice of appeal). 
Other portions of Mother's Motion for Financial 
Evaluation that were resolved in the September 9, 2025 Order are timely challenged. We now turn to those arguments.

(4) Mother argues that the Family Court improperly 
modified Father's child support obligation because the relevant statute only permits review and modification of child support 
every three years, and the party seeking a modification must 
show a "change in circumstances."  Mother contends that Father failed to show a change in circumstances justifying a modification, and that even if Father demonstrated a change in 
circumstances, the Family Court failed to use the 2024 Hawaiʻ i 
Child Support Guidelines (Guidelines) when it modified Father's 
child support obligation. Mother further argues that even if the Family Court properly utilized the Guidelines, Father failed to establish "exceptional circumstances warrant[ing] departure." 
"The family court possesses wide discretion, and its 
decisions are evaluated under the 'abuse of discretion'

 standard." KP v. EM, 157 Hawaiʻ i 35, 60, 575 P.3d 668, 693 
(2025). "A family court's findings of fact are reviewed under 
the 'clearly erroneous' standard." Id. 
HRS § 571-52.5 (2018) provides that "[w]hen the court 
establishes or modifies the amount of child support required to be paid by a parent, the court shall use the guidelines 
established under section 576D-7, except when exceptional 
circumstances warrant departure." When determining if an exceptional circumstance exists to deviate from a child support 
amount calculated using the 
Hawaiʻi Child Support Guidelines 
Worksheet (Worksheet
), the Hawaiʻ i Supreme Court has held that 
"exceptional circumstances do not excuse a failure to use the Guidelines Worksheet." P.O. v. J.S., 139 Hawaiʻ i 434, 443, 393 
P.3d 986, 995 (2017). The Court concluded: 
The Guidelines likewise permit "deviation" based on 
exceptional circumstances, and the Guidelines' requirement 
that the family court make factual findings on the "amount 
of support that would have been required as calculated using [the Guidelines Worksheet]" further demonstrates that 
even in cases of exceptional circumstances, the court must 
first calculate a support amount utilizing the Guidelines 
Worksheet. 
Id. at 444, 393 P.3d at 996 (citation omitted). 
The record on appeal does not reflect that the Family 
Court used the Worksheet when terminating Father's child support 
obligation for an indefinite period. The Family Court's stated intent — to offset Father having covered Mother's share of the Parent Coordinator's fees — did not exempt the Family Court from completing a Worksheet before terminating Father's child support obligation. See D.L. v. C.L., NO. CAAP-19-0000023, 2020 WL 
888335, at *9 (Haw. App. Feb. 24, 2020) (mem. op.).  This is especially the case here, where the Family Court did not simply offset Mother's share of the fees, but terminated child support (subject to possibly revisiting the issue at an undefined future
