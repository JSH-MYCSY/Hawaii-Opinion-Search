  II. DISCUSSION 
A. Ownership of the Property and Issue Preclusion 
At the outset, many of Gilliam's arguments are made on 
behalf of PR1 or are based on his claimed ownership interest in 
the Property.  In Gilliam's sixth point of error, which we address first, it appears that Gilliam argues the bankruptcy decisions do not preclude him from claiming an interest in PR1 
or the Property.  He seems to argue that because one of the 
bankruptcy cases was dismissed, and because this court previously remanded this case, the Order Appointing Receiver has been invalidated. Gilliam's arguments lack merit. 
First, the Order Appointing Receiver was not 
invalidated by this court's previous ruling. This court vacated the 2019 Order and 2019 Writ because those documents named Gilliam personally, even though he was not a party to the foreclosure action. Ass'n of Apartment Owners of Kuhio Shores 
at Poipu, 2024 WL 1656266, at **2-3.  However, this court's 2024 Order vacated only "the 'Order Granting Plaintiff [AOAO]'s Motion for Instructions for the Receiver' and the 'Writ of Possession,' entered on October 2, 2019." Id. at *3. Our 2024 
Order did not vacate the Order Appointing Receiver. Id. 
Second, Gilliam has presented no argument to support 
his position that 11 U.S.C. § 349 vacates the prior Bankruptcy Court's rulings. Gilliam does not explain which orders, judgments, or transfers were vacated by this statute and how each order, judgment, or transfer is related to 11 U.S.C. § 349. As such, this argument is waived. HRAP Rule 28(b)(7) (eff. 2022) ("Points not argued may be deemed waived."). 
Third, and most importantly, issue preclusion applies. 
Where there is a prior, federal court ruling on an issue, the application of issue preclusion in future state court actions is reviewed pursuant to federal law. See Int'l Bhd. of Elec. 
7
           NOT FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAIʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
 
 
 Workers, Loc. 1357 v. Hawaiian Tel. Co., 68 Haw. 316, 331 n.17,  
713 P.2d 943, 955 n.17 (1986) ("In a subsequent state court  
action, the collateral estoppel effect of a federal law ruling 
in a prior federal court adjudication is a question of federal law." (citing Limbach v. Hooven & Allison Co., 466 U.S. 353,  
359–63 (1984))).   Under federal law, for issue preclusion to  
preclude the relitigation of an issue:  "(1) the issue must be 
identical to one alleged in prior litigation; (2) the issue must 
have been 'actually litigated' in the prior litigation; and (3)  the determination of the issue in the prior litigation must 
have been 'critical and necessary' to the judgment." Beauchamp v. Anaheim Union High Sch. Dist., 816 F.3d 1216, 1225 (9th Cir. 2016) (citing  Clark v. Bear Stearns & Co., 966 F.2d 1318, 1320 
(9th Cir. 1992)).  All three requirements are met here.  
(1) Identical issues: The issue of whether Gilliam 
owned the Property or had an interest in PR1 is the same issue  
evaluated by the Bankruptcy Courts.  See In re Pac. Rim Prop.  
Serv. Corp., No. BAP HI-20-1071-BTL, 2020 WL 4371106, at *3 (B.A.P. 9th Cir. July 29, 2020) ("Gilliam is not [PR1], nor does he have any interest in or control over [PR1] or its assets   
including the [Property]."); In re Gilliam, No. CV 20-00194 JMS-WRP, 2020 WL 5848340, at *5 (D. Haw. Oct. 1, 2020) (citation  
omitted) ("A title report plainly indicates that [PR1] (not  
Gilliam) is the 'fee owner' of the [Property] . . . .      There is 
no evidence that the [Property] was ever transferred from [PR1]  
to [PR2] and then to Gilliam, much less that it was transferred  
from [PR1] directly to Gilliam."),  aff'd, 859 F. App'x 832 (9th  
Cir. 2021).  
(2) Actually litigated: This issue was "actually 
litigated" in both bankruptcy actions. See In re Gilliam  , 2020 
WL 5848340, at *3, *5 (noting Gilliam's claims in the Chapter 13  
case that he owned the Property or "was the sole shareholder of 
8
           NOT FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAIʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
 
 
  
 
 
   
 
 
  
 
 
  
 
 
 [PR1]" and rejecting these claims); In re Pac. Rim Prop. Serv. Corp., 2020 WL 4371106, at **2-3 (dismissing Gilliam's arguments in the Chapter 11 bankruptcy case that he had an interest in PR1 as its sole shareholder). 
(3) Critical and necessary: This issue was also 
"critical and necessary" to the outcomes of both bankruptcy actions.  See In re Gilliam  , 2020 WL 5848340, at *5 (stating 
that the appealed orders in the Chapter 13 action all "ultimately turn on the same factual determination:  whether Gilliam owned a property interest in the [Property] as part of  
his bankruptcy estate"); In re Pac. Rim Prop. Serv. Corp., 2020 WL 4371106, at *3 (dismissing Gilliam's appeal for lack of standing).  Additionally, Gilliam already made — and lost — this 
argument in federal court. The U.S. District Court went through this same analysis, applying federal preclusion principles, and found that Gilliam is precluded from arguing that he owned (or owns) the Property or that he had (or has) an interest in PR1. Gilliam v. Porter McGuire Kiakona & Chow, LLP, No. CV 20-00372 JMS-KJM, 2021 WL 1681076, at *1 (D. Haw. Apr. 28, 2021) ("[T]he 
Bankruptcy Court (twice) and this court have already determined 
that Plaintiff has no ownership interest in the Property or in the entity that holds title to it. . . . [T]he court gives preclusive effect to those prior factual determinations[.]").  The Ninth Circuit affirmed the U.S. District Court's judgment.  Gilliam v. Porter McGuire Kiakona & Chow, LLP, No. 21-16097, 2022 WL 17175069, at *1 (9th Cir. Nov. 23, 2022). 
In sum: Gilliam is collaterally estopped from making 
any argument that he has an ownership interest of any kind in the Property and/or PR1.

       B. Personal Jurisdiction 
Gilliam argues that the Circuit Court lacked personal 
jurisdiction over him and PR1 because they were not properly served with the FAC.  The AOAO responds that this issue is outside the scope of the current appeal. 
This court has jurisdiction to consider Gilliam's 
argument. Pursuant to the collateral order doctrine, a party 
can appeal an order to prevent an immediate transfer of property 
where that transfer would cause irreparable harm to the losing party if that party was required to wait until final judgment to appeal. See In re Maui Fire Cases, No. SCAP-26-0000484, 2026 WL 
2936776, at **4-5 (Haw. Sept. 30, 2026) (discussing the collateral order doctrine). Because we have jurisdiction over this appeal, we can review the Circuit Court's jurisdiction over the parties. See Bank of Am., N.A. v. Webb, No. CAAP-16-
0000469, 2019 WL 1785047, at **2-3 (App. Apr. 24, 2019) (SDO). 
Gilliam's arguments regarding personal jurisdiction, 
however, are without merit. Any challenge based on insufficient service of process was waived when Gilliam filed counterclaims against the AOAO. See Hanalei, BRC Inc. v. Porter, 7 Haw. App. 
304, 312, 760 P.2d 676, 681 (App. 1988) ("[W]here a defense of lack of personal jurisdiction is joined with a permissive counterclaim or a voluntary affirmative claim, the defense is waived."). As for Gilliam's argument that the AOAO failed to serve PR1, this contention lacks merit: Robinson's counsel accepted service on behalf of Robinson, who (as the Receiver) was the proper recipient of service for PR1. See HRS § 414-
413(c) (2004) (providing in relevant part that "[a]mong other powers[,] . . . [t]he receiver . . . [m]ay sue and defend in the receiver's own name as receiver of the corporation in all courts of this State" and "[t]he custodian may exercise all of the

 powers of the corporation").  Gilliam's arguments regarding 
personal jurisdiction are without merit. 
C. Subject Matter Jurisdiction 
Gilliam appears to challenge the Circuit Court's 
subject matter jurisdiction based on his contention that the AOAO improperly assessed fees against PR1.  Specifically, Gilliam appears to argue that PR1 was not a member of the 
homeowner's association because it does not own the Property and 
cannot now take ownership of the Property, such that the common expense assessments levied against the Property by the AOAO were improper. He also argues that any collection against PR1 is improper under HRS § 415-105, and that pursuant to HRS § 415-99, a receiver must be appointed with bond. 
We find no error. First, as discussed supra, Gilliam 
is precluded from making arguments that PR1 is not the owner of the Property. Second, he may not assert defenses on behalf of the owner of the Property, because he lacks any interest in such defenses.

Gilliam also asserts a violation of HRS § 415-99, but 
his argument regarding this statute is unclear. It appears he 
is again attempting to make arguments on behalf of PR1.

D. Unclean Hands 
Gilliam argues that many of the fees amounting to a 
lien against the Property are improper. Once again, Gilliam may not challenge these fees, as he has no interest in PR1 or the 
Property. 
E. Motion for Appointment of Receiver 
Gilliam next argues that the Circuit Court improperly 
granted the AOAO's Motion for Appointment of Receiver. His 
arguments on this point are unclear. He appears to argue that the Circuit Court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, that the AOAO lacked standing to request the appointment of the Receiver, that there was insufficient service of process on PR1, and that the AOAO's filing of the Motion for Appointment of Receiver asserted a new claim for relief against a new party that therefore required leave to amend. 
Whether Gilliam preserved these arguments at the trial 
court level is similarly unclear; nevertheless, the arguments 
fail. 
Again, Gilliam has no interest in PR1 or the Property, 
and may not raise defenses potentially available to PR1 but not 
applicable to him.  Furthermore, the circuit courts have subject matter jurisdiction to appoint a receiver pursuant to 
HRS § 414-422(a) (2004), and 
Hawai‘i courts sitting in equity 
have the independent power to appoint equitable receiverships. 
See Hawaii Ventures, LLC v. Otaka, Inc.
, 114 Hawai‘i 438, 456, 
164 P.3d 696, 714 (2007).      
Regarding Gilliam's argument that the Motion for 
Receiver constituted a new claim that required leave to amend, 
Gilliam cannot make this argument as he has no interest in PR1 and cannot make arguments on its behalf. 
Even if he could make 
12           NOT FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAIʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
 this claim, Gilliam does not make any discernable argument as to 
how the Circuit Court abused its discretion by appointing a receiver. 
F. 
Special Motion to Dismiss and Stay 
Gilliam alleges that the Circuit Court violated HRS 
chapter 634G by failing to stay proceedings upon Gilliam's filing of his SMTD. Gilliam's SMTD, however, did not make any 
argument as to how chapter 634G was applicable to the current 
case. This argument is waived. 
G. Hearing on Motion for Instructions 
Gilliam's last argument appears to be that the Circuit 
Court erred in ruling on the Motion for Instructions because (i) erroneous exhibits were attached to the motion and (ii) the Circuit Court denied Gilliam due process by disallowing him to call a witness at the hearing. Both of these issues relate to the debts of PR1 and, as such, cannot be brought by Gilliam. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the foregoing reasons, we affirm the October 20, 
2025 "Order Granting Amended Receiver's Motion for Instructions Regarding Sale of Property" and accompanying "Writ of 
Possession." Gilliam has also filed numerous additional motions 
that are currently pending before this court. All such motions are hereby denied.
