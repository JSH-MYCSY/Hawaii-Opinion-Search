  Upon careful review of the record and the briefs 
submitted by the parties, and having given due consideration to  
the issues raised and the arguments advanced, we resolve this 
appeal as discussed below and affirm.    
At trial, Hale Koa Hotel Assistant Manager Anthony 
Sandstrom 
and Honolulu Police Department (HPD) Officer Cyler 
Solomon (Officer Solomon) testified on behalf of Plaintiff-
Appellee State of Hawaiʻi. 
Sandstrom testified that Hale Koa sits on Fort 
DeRussy, which is a military base, and is a hotel for military 
members or those with military affiliation. 
In August 2024, 
Sandstrom was called to the lobby to attend to a situation with Hughes. Sandstrom repeatedly asked Hughes for identification or to show he was a guest of the hotel, but Hughes "wouldn't answer." Sandstrom then called HPD. 
Officer Solomon testified that he arrived to find 
Hughes laying on the ground. Officer Solomon asked Hughes "what's going on," and Hughes replied that "his foot is broken."  Officer Solomon called for emergency medical services (EMS). 
While waiting for EMS, Sandstrom issued Hughes a "trespass 
warning document." 
When EMS arrived, they parked in the valet driveway 
area, and Officer Solomon and Hughes (among others) walked over to meet EMS. Officer Solomon testified that Hughes was limping

(1) Hughes challenges the sufficiency of the 
evidence, arguing there was insufficient evidence to establish 
that he was on the premises of a hotel and that he acted 
knowingly. 
"[E]vidence adduced in the trial court must be 
considered in the strongest light for the prosecution when the appellate court passes on the legal sufficiency of such evidence 
to support a conviction . . . ."  State v. Kalaola, 124 Hawaiʻ i 
43, 49, 237 P.3d 1109, 1115 (2010) (alteration in original) (quoting State v. Richie, 88 Hawaiʻ i 19, 33, 960 P.2d 1227, 1241

 (1998)). "The test on appeal is not whether guilt is 
established beyond a reasonable doubt, but whether there was substantial evidence to support the conclusion of the trier of 
fact." Id. (quoting 
Richie, 88 Hawaiʻ i at 33, 960 P.2d at 1241). 
"Substantial evidence as to every material element of the 
offense charged is credible evidence which is of sufficient quality and probative value to enable a person of reasonable caution to support a conclusion." Id. (citation modified). 
(a) Premises of a hotel 
Hughes argues the State "failed to prove" he "was ever 
inside a 'structure' constituting a hotel." Hughes asserts that 
he was never in the towers where the "roomers and boarders" are; 
he was in the open-air lobby and valet driveway area.  Hughes further asserts that "the evil the legislature sought to remedy was the uncontrolled movement of criminal actors through the interior of hotel and apartment buildings, enclosed spaces where guests reside, sleep, and are entitled to security and privacy." 
As relevant to this case, "[a] person commits the 
offense of criminal trespass in the first degree if . . . [t]hat 
person knowingly enters or remains unlawfully . . . [i]n or upon 
the premises of a hotel."  HRS § 708-813(1)(a)(ii) (emphases added). To "enter or remain unlawfully" means 
to enter or remain in or upon premises when the person is
not licensed, invited, or otherwise privileged to do so. A 
person who, regardless of the person's intent, enters or
remains in or upon premises which are at the time open to
the public does so with license and privilege unless the 
4   NOT FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST'S HAWAIʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER 
 
 
  
 
 
  
  
 
 
   
  
      
   
 
   
   
 
  
 person defies a lawful order not to enter or remain, 
personally communicated to the person by the owner of the premises or some other authorized person. 
HRS § 708-800 (2014) (emphases added). A hotel is "a structure 
in which a majority of the tenants are roomers or boarders." HRS § 708-800. And premises "includes any building and any real property."  HRS § 708-800. 
Hughes attempts to distinguish between the towers 
where the guest rooms are located and other parts of the hotel, such as the open-air lobby and the valet driveway area.  However, the "premises" of a hotel "includes any building and 
any real property." See HRS § 708-800 (emphasis added). 
Sandstrom testified that the property consists of 72 
acres and includes two towers with 800 rooms and "a circle 
round-about with an open-air lobby." Both Sandstrom and Officer Solomon testified that Hughes was in the open-air lobby.  The district court found their testimony credible. See State v. 
Pulse, 83 Hawaiʻ i 229, 244, 925 P.2d 797, 812 (1996) ("The 
testimony of one percipient witness can provide sufficient evidence to support a conviction."). 
When viewing the testimony of Sandstrom and Officer 
Solomon in the light most favorable to the prosecution, 
sufficient evidence existed to support the district court's 
conclusion that Hughes was on the premises of a hotel.

 (b) Knowingly state of mind 
Hughes argues the State "failed to adduce substantial 
evidence that [he] acted knowingly." Relying on State v. 
Quizon
, Hughes specifically argues the State did not adduce 
sufficient evidence that "Hughes knew he was in a structure in which a majority of the tenants [were] roomers or boarders."  
See 136 Hawaiʻ i 28, 356 P.3d 1048, No. CAAP-14-0000752, 2015 WL 
5252397, at *2 (App. Sep. 8, 2015) (SDO). 
In Quizon, this court noted that, although the 
defendant was given a trespass warning, the "warning erroneously 
referred to second-degree trespass upon a 'commercial premises,' 
instead of first-degree trespass upon a hotel. Therefore, the 
trespass warning did not provide evidence that [the defendant] knew that the YWCA Fernhurst was a hotel." Id. at *2 (emphasis 
added). 
"A person acts knowingly with respect to attendant 
circumstances when he is aware that such circumstances exist." HRS § 702-206(2)(b) (2014).  And we may draw reasonable inferences from the circumstances surrounding the defendant's 
conduct: 
Given the difficulty of proving the requisite state of mind 
by direct evidence in criminal cases, we have consistently held that . . . proof by circumstantial evidence and
reasonable inferences arising from circumstances surrounding the defendant's conduct is sufficient. . . . 
Thus, the mind of an alleged offender may be read from hisacts, conduct and inferences fairly drawn from all the circumstances.

 State v. Batson
, 73 Haw. 236, 254, 831 P.2d 924, 934 (1992) 
(citation modified). 
As Hughes notes, the trespass warning document refers 
to the United States Code, but it also refers to HRS § 708-800 
and the definition of "premises." The trespass warning document states that "[t]he premises you are trespassed from includes but is not limited to the following within the real property of the aforementioned business: rooms, parking lot, garages, lobby, 
pool, recreation area, loading dock, guest floors, stairwells, 
restrooms, rooftops, any concessions or restaurants within the property." The trespass warning document also states that Hughes was "trespassed from the Hale Koa Hotel". 
Unlike Quizon, the trespass warning document did not 
"erroneously refer[] to second-degree trespass upon a 'commercial premises,' instead of first-degree trespass upon a hotel." See 2015 WL 5252397, at *2. And "Hale Koa Hotel" was 
repeated four times on the trespass warning document. 
Even if the trespass warning document could not be 
used as evidence to establish Hughes's state of mind, other evidence established that Hughes was aware he was on the premises of a hotel. Sandstrom testified that he approached Hughes in the center of the lobby, and he believed Hughes was sitting on one of the sofas in the lobby. Sandstrom asked if he was a "hotel" guest, repeatedly.  Officer Solomon also testified

  that Hughes was in the lobby, and when EMS arrived, Hughes 
walked from the lobby to the valet driveway area. It is reasonable to infer from Sandstrom and Officer Solomon's testimonies that Hughes was aware he was on the premises of a hotel. See Batson

Thus, sufficient evidence supported the district

(2) Hughes contends the "district court failed to 
consider the choice of evils defense under HRS § 703-302 despite evidence that [he] did not leave the Fort DeRussy property because of a foot injury." (Formatting altered.) Hughes argues that his "conduct of being a non-violent trespasser was the lesser evil to aggravating his foot injury by leaving a 72-acre property on foot." However, Hughes acknowledges that he did not raise HRS § 703-302 (2014) explicitly below. 
HRS § 703-302 provides in relevant part: 
(1) Conduct which the actor believes to be 
necessary to avoid an imminent harm or evil to the actor or 
to another is justifiable provided that: 
(a) The harm or evil sought to be avoided by such
conduct is greater than that sought to be
prevented by the law defining the offense charged; 
(b) Neither the Code nor other law defining the
offense provides exceptions or defenses dealing
with the specific situation involved; and 
(c) A legislative purpose to exclude the justification claimed does not otherwise
plainly appear. 
HRS § 703-302(1).

 Contrary to Hughes's contention, the district court 
considered his foot injury. 
The district court found that 
Hughes was given medical attention, and based on the BWC video 
admitted into evidence, Hughes "was able to walk." Moreover, the district court found that the BWC video "showed that the EMS worker indicated that [Hughes] was able to walk, that the foot was not swollen, and that he could not see anything wrong with the foot that he complained of." Accordingly, the district 
court found unpersuasive "any arguments that he was not able to 
walk or leave the property . . . because there was credible evidence that he could . . . and did in fact walk out to the EMS worker who was out there." 
In sum, the evidence presented, as weighed by the 
district court, showed that the "harm or evil sought to be avoided" (aggravating a purported foot injury) was not "greater than that sought to be prevented" (trespassing). HRS § 703-
302(1); see HRS § 641-16 (2016) (providing in part that there 
shall be no reversal in a criminal case "for any finding depending on the credibility of witnesses or the weight of the 
evidence"); State v. Eastman
, 81 Hawaiʻi 131, 139, 913 P.2d 57, 
65 (1996) ("An appellate court will not pass upon the trial 
judge's decisions with respect to the credibility of witnesses

the trial judge.").
