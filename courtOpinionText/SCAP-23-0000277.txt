I. Introduction 
Both the plaintiffs and defendants appeal a circuit court 
final judgment in this transfer case. 
A.H. (pseudonym “Amy”) has severe cognitive disabilities 
and lacks capacity to consent to sex. While a student at 
McKinley High School (“McKinley”) , Amy was sexually abused by 
male students . The abuse eventually caused her to suffer a 
psychotic breakdown and sustain serious, continuing 
psychological injur ies. 
Amy’s mother (“Mother”) filed this negligence lawsuit on 
behalf of Amy and herself ( collectively , “Plaintiffs”) in the 
Circuit Court of the First Circuit (“circuit court”) . The State 
of Hawai ‘i, its Department of Education (“DOE”), and DOE 
employees (collectively , “the State”) were named as defendants . 
Plaintiffs alleged that the State negligently failed to protect 
Amy from repeated sexual abuse while she attended McKinley. 
The circuit court presided over a sixty -six-day bench 
trial. There was extensive evidence that school personnel knew

  of Amy’s significant cognitive disabilities, recognized her 
extraordinary vulnerability to sexual exploitation, were 
repeatedly confronted with warning signs that she was being 
sexually abused, but failed to take reasonable measures to 
prevent it. 
The circuit court concluded that the State negligently 
failed to protect Amy. It determined that Plaintiffs ’ injuries 
and requisite treatment merited a total damages award of more 
than $14 million , more than half for special damages . But 
without any request from the State that it do so, in its written 
decision, the circuit court apportioned 30% of the fault to the 
male students and reduced Plaintiffs’ damages award by that 
percentage. 
This sua sponte apportionment is the main issue on appeal . 
The circuit court cited Hawai‘i Revised Statutes (“HRS”) § 
663-10.5 (Supp. 2017) as the basis for this apportionment. That 
statute provides in relevant part: 
§663-10.5 Government entity as a tortfeasor; abolition of 
joint and several liability. (a) Any other law to the 
contrary notwithstanding, . . . in any case where a 
government entity is determined to be a tortfeasor along 
with one or more other tortfeasors, the government entity 
shall be liable for no more than that percentage share of 
the damages attributable to the government entity [.] 
(b) For purposes of this section, the liability of a 
government entity shall include its vicarious liability 
for the acts or omissions of its officers and 
employees. 
(c) For purposes of this section, “government entity” means 
any unit of government in this State, including: 
(1) The State[.]

 Plaintiffs contend the circuit court improperly reduced 
their recovery when the State never pursued apportionment of 
liability to the male students . The State pled HRS § 663 -10.5 
as a defense in its answer but never argued that the male 
students should bear a portion of liability. Rather, in its 
closing argument and proposed findings and conclusions, the 
State briefly argued that if the State was found liable, then 
part of the liability should be apportioned to Mother. 
Specifically, t he State asked the circuit court to reduce 
the Plaintiffs’ damages by Mother’s alleged negligen ce in 
failing to provide more information to the school. The circuit 
court rejected this argument but then, on its own accord, 
allocated liability to the male students . 
On Plaintiffs ’ points of error on appeal , we hold that the 
circuit court’s sua sponte allocation of fault to the male 
students violated Plaintiffs’ Hawaiʻi constitutional due process 
rights. Plaintiffs were deprived of notice and an opportunity 
to be heard on whether the boys were actually “other 
tortfeasors ” under HRS § 663 -10.5 and, if so, what percentage, 
if any, should be allocated to them. We set out what steps due 
process requires for an “other tortfeasor” determination. 
We also hold that there was insufficient evidence that the 
male students qualified as “other tortfeasors” under HRS § 663 -
10.5, especially because the circuit court explicitly found

Finally, based on these holdings, we also hold that the

III. Standards of Review 
A. Findings of Fact 
HRCP Rule 52(a) provides that, in a bench trial, 
“[f]indings of fact shall not be set aside unless clearly 
erroneous, and due regard shall be given to the opportunity of 
the trial court to judge of the credibility of the witnesses.” 
HRCP Rule 52(a). A finding of fact is clearly erroneous when 
“(1) the record lack s substantial evidence to support the 
finding, or (2) despite substantial evidence in support of the 
finding, the appellate court is nonetheless left with a definite 
and firm conviction that a mistake has been made.” Klink v. 
36  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
   
  State, 113  Hawai‘i 332, 351, 152 P.3d 504, 523 (2007). 
“‘Substantial evidence’ . . . is credible evidence which is of 
sufficient quality and probative value to enable a person of reasonable caution to support a conclusion.” Id.  (cleaned up).  
B. Conclusions of Law 
“This court ‘may freely review conclusions of law and the 
applicable standard of review is the right/wrong test.’” State 
v. Levi, 102 Hawai‘i 282, 285, 75 P.3d 1173, 1176 (2003). As 
such, a conclusion of law is not binding upon an appellate court 
and such findings can be “freely review[ed]” for its 
correctness. Dan v. State, 76 Hawaiʻ i  423, 425, 879 P.2d 528, 
530 (1994). Questions of statutory interpretation and 
constitutional law are likewise questions of law reviewed de 
novo. Levi, 102 Hawai ‘i at 285, 75 P.3d at 1176.  
C. Statutory Interpretation 
“The fundamental starting point for statutory 
interpretation is the language of the statute itself.” Peterson 
v. Haw. Elec. Light Co., 85 Hawai‘i 322, 327, 944 P.2d 1265, 1270 
(1997), superseded on other grounds by  HRS § 269-15.5 (Supp. 
1999) (cleaned up).  “[W]here the statutory language is plain 
and unambiguous, the court’s sole duty is to give effect to its 
plain and obvious meaning.” Id.  (cleaned up ). “[I]mplicit  in 
the task of statutory construction is the court’s foremost obligation to ascertain and give effect to the intention of the 
37  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
  
 
 
 
  legislature, which is to be obtained primarily from the language 
contained in the statute itself.” Id. (citation omitted). 
“[W]hen there is doubt, doubleness of meaning, or 
indistinctiveness or uncertainty of an expression used in a 
statute, an ambiguity exists.” 85 Hawai‘i 322 at 327-28, 944 
P.2d at 1270 -71 (cleaned up ). 
When a statute is ambiguous, “the meaning of the ambiguous 
words may be sought by examining the context, with which the 
ambiguous words, phrases, and sentences may be compared, in 
order to ascertain their true meaning.” Citizens Against 
Reckless Dev. v. Zoning Bd. of Appeals , 114 Hawai‘i 184, 194, 159 
P.3d 143, 153 (2007) (quoting HRS § 1 -15(1) (1993)). Courts may 
also “resort to extrinsic aids in determining legislative 
intent, such as legislative history, or the reason and spirit of 
the law.” Id. 
D. Discovery Rulings 
The extent to which discovery is permitted under Rule 26 
(as well as under Rules 33 and 34) is subject to considerable 
latitude and discretio n. Appeal of Goodfader , 45 Haw. 317, 335, 
367 P.2d 472, 483 (1961) . Accordingly, “the trial court may not 
be reversed by an appellate court unless the trial court has 
clearly exceeded the bounds of reason or disregarded rules or 
principles of law or practice to the substantial detriment of a 
party litigant.” Kealoha v. Cnty. of Haw. , 74 Haw. 308, 318, 
38  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
   
 
  
 
   
 844 P.2d 670, 675 (1993)  (cleaned up). Under that standard,  
different trial judges may, on the same facts, arrive at 
opposite rulings “without any of them being reversible on 
appeal.” State v. Rabe, 5 Haw. App. 251, 260-61, 687 P.2d 554, 
561 (App. 1984).  
To the extent this issue requires review of the circuit 
court’s factual findings regarding Plaintiffs’  medical condition 
and ability to participate in discovery, those findings are “reviewed on appeal under the clearly erroneous standard.” 
Birano v. State, 143 
Hawai‘i 163, 181, 426 P.3d 387, 405 (2018) 
(cleaned up).  
E. Damages Awards 
Hawai‘i law has long recognized that the assessment of 
damages lies primarily within the province of the factfinder, 
and appellate review is correspondingly limited. See Kang v. 
Harrington , 59 Haw. 652, 663, 587 P.2d 285, 292 (1978). Most 
recently, this court reaffirmed these longstanding principles in 
Kekona v. Bornemann and explained: 
On appeal, the courts analyze whether the damages awarded 
by the jury were palpably not supported by the evidence, or 
so excessive and outrageous when considered with the 
circumstances of the case as to demonstrate that the jury 
in assessing damages acted against rules of law or suffered 
their passions or prejudices to mislead them. 
135 Hawaiʻi 254, 257, 349 P.3d 361, 364 (2015). 
This court has further explained that “[a] similar test is 
used in a jury-waived case,” where “the inquiry on review is 
39  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
  
  
 
 
   
 
  
 
 
 
 
   
 limited to whether, ‘upon the evidence adduced, reasonable 
[people] could have come to the same conclusion as the jury, or 
the trial court in a jury-waived case.’”   Kang, 59 Haw. at 663, 
587 P.2d at 292 (cleaned up).

b.  Due process requires notice and a meaningful

Article I, Section 5 of the Hawaiʻi Constitution provides 
that “[n]o person shall be deprived of life, liberty or property 
without due process of law.” Haw. Const. Art. I, § 5. 
Procedural due process “protect[s] individuals against the 
State’s arbitrary deprivation of life, liberty, or property.” 
Brown v. Thompson, 91 Hawaiʻ i 1, 11, 979 P.2d 586, 596 (1999).

We agree. 
i. Plaintiffs have a property interest . 
In a due process analysis, Hawai‘i courts first ask whether 
the particular interest which the claimant seeks to protect is 
“‘property’ within the meaning of the due process clauses.” 91 
Hawaiʻi at 10, 979 P.2d at 595 ( cleaned up ). “[A] benefit which 
one is entitled to receive by statute constitutes a 
constitutionally -protected property interest.” 91 Hawaiʻi at 10-
11, 979 P.2d at 595-96 (cleaned up ). The United States Supreme 
Court has recognized that a state -law cause of action is a 
47  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
   
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 protected property interest for purposes of the federal Due 
Process Clause. See  Logan v. Zimmerman Brush Co., 455 U.S. 422, 
428 (1982)  (citing Martinez v. California, 444 U.S. 277, 281-82 
(1980) (observing that a state tort claim is a “species of 
‘property’ protected by the Due Process Clause”)).    
We hold that Plaintiffs also have a due process property 
interest under Article I, Section 5 in their cause of action as 
well as in their damages award. The circuit court determined 
that Plaintiffs were entitled to $ 14,167,354.81  in damages. The 
circuit court then reduced that recovery by 30%, depriving 
Plaintiffs of $ 4,250,206.44 . Because that ruling extinguished a 
portion of Plaintiffs’ adjudicated recovery, it implicated a 
protected property interest that could not be taken without 
constitutionally adequate procedures. 
ii. Hawai‘i’s due process clause requires 
procedures that are fundamentally fair 
before a protected property interest may be 
deprived . 
Because Plaintiffs possess a protected property interest in 
their damages award , this court must determine what procedures 
were constitutionally “required to protect it.” Brown, 91 
Hawai‘i at 10, 979 P.2d at 595.  Procedural due process is not “a 
fixed concept requiring a specific procedural course in every 
situation.” Id. (cleaned up ). Rather, “due process is flexible 
and calls for such procedural protections as the particular

 situation demands.” Id. (cleaned up ). Nevertheless, “[t]he 
basic elements of procedural due process of law require notice 
and an opportunity to be heard at a meaningful time and in a 
meaningful manner.” Id. (cleaned up ). 
The legislative history of HRS § 663 -10.5 said that the 
measure was necessary to promote “fairness and equity for 
government entities” that, because of their perceived “deep 
pockets,” might otherwise be required to pay more than their 
fair share when determined to be joint tortfeasors. It can be

governmental entities. But , as noted, Plaintiffs are also

To determine the specific procedures required to satisfy

including (1) the private interest that will be affected; (2) 
the risk of an erroneous deprivation of that interest through 
the procedures actually used, and the probable value, if any, of 
additional or alternative procedural safeguards; and (3) the 
governmental interest, including the burden that additional 
procedural safeguards would entail. Id. 
First, the private interest affected was significant: the

  was to not require any notice or opportunity to be heard at all.

a. Apportionment of liability to a non -party “other

Preliminarily, Plaintiffs assert that HRS § 663-10.5 
creates an affirmative defense. The State disagrees.  This 
court has not addressed whether or not HRS § 663-10.5 is an 
affirmative defense for a government entity asserting “other

 tortfeasor” liability . This is a question affecting pleading 
and proof burdens. 
HRCP Rule 8(c) requires that “[i ]n pleading to a preceding 
pleading, a party shall set forth affirmatively” numerous 
enumerated defenses, including contributory negligence, release, 
waiver, and the statute of limitations, “and any other matter 
constituting an avoidance or affirmative def ense.” The 
affirmative defenses listed in the rule are not exclusive.  Rule 
8(c) expressly provides that “‘any other matters constituting an avoidance or affirmative defense’ must also be pleaded 
affirmatively.” Touche Ross, Ltd. v. Filipek, 7 Haw. App. 473,  
487  778 P.2d 721, 730  (App. 1989).  
Generally, an affirmative defense is one that defeats or 
bars an otherwise valid claim. Bhakta v. Cnty . of Maui, 109 
Hawai‘i 198, 211, 124 P.3d 943, 956 (2005). But affirmative 
defenses are not limited to defenses that completely bar 
recovery. 
For example, HRCP Rule 8(c) identifies “payment” as an 
affirmative defense. Black’s Law Dictionary  defines “payment” 
as “[p]erformance  of an obligation by the delivery of money or 
some other valuable thing accepted in partial or full discharge of the obligation.” Payment, Black’s Law Dictionary  (12th ed. 
2024).   So partial payment is an affirmative defense.  
56  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
  
   
 
 
 
 
 Likewise, HRCP Rule 8(c) identifies contributory negligence 
as an affirmative defense. Under Hawai ‘i law, contributory 
negligence is no longer a complete bar to recovery and has been 
replaced by comparative negligence, which reduces a plaintiff’s 
recovery when the plaintiff’s negligence is not greater than the 
combined negligence of the defendants. HRS § 663-31(c). Both 
contributory and comparative negligence are affirmative 
defenses. 
The State contends that HRS § 663 -10.5 is not an 
affirmative defense and is merely a limitation on liability. It 
cites no authority for that proposition and does not explain why 
HRS § 663 -10.5 should be treated differently from the numerous 
affirmative defenses that limit or reduce liability. 
Also, the State appears to have recognized HRS § 663 -10.5 
is an affirmative defense because it pled it as such. The 
fifteenth defense in the State’s answer to the first amended 
complaint says: “The liability, if any, of the DOE, Nakagawa and 
Jones, any of them or collectively, is limited under HRS §663 -
10.5.” The fourteenth defense preceding this defense alleges 
that Plaintiffs’ claims are barred by the equitable doctrines of 
estoppel, collateral estoppel, res judicata, laches, and unclean 
hands, which are affirmative defenses. 
Other jurisdictions addressing nonparty apportionment 
generally recognize that reducing a defendant’s liability by

defense.  Courts have therefore required defendants to provide 
timely notice and bear the burden of proving the nonparty’s 
fault.  
For example, in Hardin v. Manitowoc-Forsythe Corp., 691 
F.2d 449, 453 (10th Cir. 1982), the Tenth Circuit addressed 
whether fault could be allocated to “phantom parties” under 
Kansas law. In discussing the procedural effect of the 
defendants’ failure to plead phantom -party fault, the court 
explained that  “[t]he policy behind Rule 8(c) is to put 
plaintiff on notice well in advance of trial that defendant 
intends to present a defense in the nature of an avoidance.”   
691 F.2d at 458. It then characterized phantom-party fault as 
such a defense, stating that “[t]he defense that a phantom party 
contributed to the plaintiff’s injuries is in the nature of an 
avoidance and normally would be required to be pleaded 
affirmatively in the answer.” Id.   
Likewise, in McGraw v. Sanders Co. Plumbing & Heating , 
Inc., 667 P.2d 289, 295-96 (Kan. 1983), the Kansas Supreme Court 
held that a defendant seeking to reduce its percentage of fault 
by attributing fault to another occupies the same burden as the 
defendant who claims the plaintiff was partially or entirely at 
fault. 667 P.2d at 295.   The court reasoned that the defense 
that a “‘phantom party’ contributed to the plaintiff’s injuries

 is arguably a ‘matter constituting an avoidance’” and therefore 
places the defendant “in the same position as if he were 
claiming the plaintiff was negligent.” Id.  
The Florida Supreme Court adopted the same approach in Nash 
v. Wells Fargo Guard Serv .’s, Inc., 678 So. 2d 1262, 1264-65 
(Fla. 1996), holding that “the defendant must plead as an affirmative defense the negligence of the nonparty and 
specifically identify the nonparty.” The court explained that 
pretrial notice is essential because the assertion that 
noneconomic damages should be apportioned against a nonparty 
“may affect both the presentation of the case and the trial 
court’s rulings on evidentiary issues.” 678 So. 2d at 1264.  
Thus, we hold that where “a government entity is determined 
to be a tortfeasor” but seeks a determination that it is “liable 
for no more than that percentage share of the damages 
attributable to the government entity” due to the alleged 
liability of a nonp arty “other tortfeasor,” it is asserting an 
affirmative defense. 
b. A defendant asserting an affirmative defense 
bears the burden of pleading and proving the 
defense. 
Generally, the defendant has the burden of proof on all 
affirmative defenses, which includes the burden of proving facts which are essential to the asserted defense.   State ex rel. 
Shikada v. Bristol-Myers Squibb Co., 152 
Hawai‘i 418, 526 P.3d 
59  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
 395  (2023) (accord and satisfaction is an affirmative defense,

generally, a plaintiff is not required to disprove affirmative

313 P.3d 717, 730 (2013).

same principle.   In McGraw, the Kansas Supreme Court explained 
that a defendant seeking to reduce its percentage of fault by 
attributing fault to another  “would have the burden to plead and 
prove his claims,” relying on the general rule that “the burden of proof on any point is upon the party asserting it.” 667 P.2d

Id.  
Similarly, in Nash, the Florida Supreme Court held that  
“the defendant has the burden of presenting at trial that the 
nonparty’s fault contributed to the accident” in order to 
include the nonparty on the verdict form. 678 So. 2d at 1265.  
This court’s approach to liability apportionment is 
consistent with HRS § 663 -10.5 being an affirmative defense. In 
Collins v. Greenstein , 61 Haw. 26, 41 n.10, 595 P.2d 275, 284

 n.10 (1979), this court quoted the Restatement (Second) of Torts 
§ 433B(2), which states:  
Where the tortious conduct of two or more actors has 
combined to bring about harm to the plaintiff, and one or 
more of the actors seeks to limit his liability on the 
ground that the harm is capable of apportionment among 
them, the burden of proof as to the apportionment is upon 
each such actor. 
Although this statement appeared in a footnote, it reflects 
the same allocation of the burden applicable to affirmative 
defenses generally: when an actor seeks to limit their  own 
liability on the ground that the plaintiff’s harm is capable of 
apportionment, that actor bears the burden of proving the 
apportionment .   Thus, the burden is on the party seeking to 
reduce their  liability through apportionment, rather than 
requiring the plaintiff to disprove apportionment.  
Here, the State sought to limit and reduce its liability . 
The circuit court determined that the State was liable for 
Plaintiffs’ damages , but reduced the State’s share of liability 
by 30% based on the purported fault of other tortfeasors. 
Because the State sought to limit its liability through 
apportionment, it bore the burden of properly pleading and 
proving the facts necessary to support that reduction.

 c. The State failed to carry its burden and waived 
any apportionment to the male students . 
The State generally ple d HRS § 663 -10.5 as an affirmative 
defense. But it never specifically asserted that it was seeking 
to allocate fault to the boys under that statute. 
The circuit court’s apportionment under th e circumstances 
of this case effectively shifted the State’s burden to 
Plaintiffs. The State never identified the male students as the 
alleged “other tortfeasors” upon whom its affirmative defense 
depended . It was not Plaintiffs’ burden to prove that 
unidentified nonparties were not tortfeasors. 
Nor did the State make any attempt to establish a 
percentage o f fault attributable to the male students. The 
State did not identify the students whose fault it sought to 
allocate, present evidence directed toward establishing their 
status as “other tortfeasors” (or their respective 
responsibilit ies), or propose any percentage of fault 
attributable to them in its closing argument or proposed 
findings . 
By concluding  that “all persons who abused [Amy] sexually 
during the relevant time period” were “other tortfeasors” and 
collectively allocating  30% of the fault to them, the circuit 
court relieved the State of its burden to establish both the 
62  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
   
 
 
 
 
  
 
 
 
 tortfeasor status of the persons upon whom its affirmative 
defense depended , and the degree of fault attributable to them. 
Because the State did not attempt to carry its burden, it 
waived any argument that its liability should be reduced based 
on the male students’ alleged fault. Therefore, the circuit 
court erred in apportioning 30% of the fault to the male 
students. 
Properly p lacing the burden on the party seeking 
apportionment to establish the legal and factual basis for 
allocating fault to a nonparty ensures that a plaintiff’s 
recovery is not diminished in violation of due process, as 
occurred here. 
4. The circuit court erred in denying Plaintiffs’ motion 
to amend the judgment. 
Plaintiffs’ fourth point of error challenges the circuit

For the reasons discussed above, Plaintiffs’ motion should 
have been granted. The State did not assert that the male 
students were “other tortfeasors” under HRS § 663 -10.5, did not

degree of fault, and the circuit court could not shift that

 burden to Plaintiffs and sua sponte apportion 30% of the fault

Accordingly, the circuit court erred in denying Plaintiffs’

The State raises three points of error on cross -appeal. 
The first two concern pretrial orders that denied a HRCP Rule 35

1. The circuit court did not abuse its discretion 
by denying a further deposition of Amy. 
The State alleges that the circuit court abused its 
discretion by denying its request to complete Amy’s deposition.  
Discovery rulings are reviewed for abuse of discretion.   
Kealoha, 74 Haw. at  319, 844 P.2d at  676. Under this standard, 
we address whether the circuit court clearly exceeded the bounds 
of reason or disregarded rules or principles of law to the 
substantial detriment of the State.  Id.   In general, this court 
has  recognized that discovery rules reflect a policy favoring 
broad disclosure of relevant information and that “the 
deposition-discovery rules are to be accorded a broad and 
64  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
 liberal treatment.” Appeal of Goodfader, 45 Haw. at  336,  367 
P.2d at 472 (citation omitted). HRCP Rule 30 generally permits 
parties to obtain testimony through oral depositions. HRCP Rule 
30.   HRCP Rule 30 provides that “[a]fter commencement of the 
action, any party may take the testimony of any person, including a party, by deposition upon oral examination.”  
That right, however, is not absolute  or without parameters. 
“[D]iscovery, like all matters of procedure, has ultimate and necessary boundaries.” Appeal of Goodfader, 45 Haw. at 336, 367 
P.2d at 483  (quoting Hickman v. Taylor, 329 U.S. 495, 507 
(1947)).  For example, HRCP Rule 26  generally allows  courts to 
limit discovery where the discovery sought is “unreasonably cumulative or duplicative, or is obtainable from some other 
source that is more convenient, less burdensome, or less 
expensive”;  where the requesting party has already had ample 
opportunity to obtain the information sought, or where “the burden or expense of the proposed discovery outweighs its likely benefit.” HRCP Rule 26 (eff. 2022).  
HRCP Rule 26 also authorizes courts to issue protective 
orders to “protect a party or person from annoyance, 
embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden or expense.”  
Further, HRCP Rule 30  specifically allows for oral depositions 
to be limited “when it can be shown that the examination is 
being conducted in bad faith or in such a manner as to annoy, 
65  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
   
 embarrass or oppress the person subject to the inquiry.” HRCP 
Rule 30(d). 
This court has repeatedly recognized that trial courts  have 
considerable  latitude and discretion  over discovery  requests. 
Kilakila  ʻO  Haleakala   v.  Bd.  of  Land  &  Nat.  Res., 138 Hawaiʻ i  
383, 401, 382 P.3d 195, 213 (2016).  And in exercising that 
discretion, courts may consider “all factors, including the 
effect the inquiry might have on the person to be interrogated.” 
Appeal of Goodfader, 45 Haw. at 335, 367 P.2d at 483.  
Hawaiʻi courts have not articulated a specific framework 
governing when a trial court may limit further testimony from a 
medically or psychologically vulnerable witness. 
However, Hawaiʻi courts have recognized a close relationship 
between HRCP Rule 26 and its federal counterpart. See  Brende v. 
Hara, 113 Hawai ʻi 424, 431, 153 P.3d 1109, 1116 (2007) (observing 
that HRCP Rule 26(c) is “patterned after Federal Rules of Civil 
Procedure Rule 26(c) ” and “[i]n instances where Hawai ʻi case law 
and statutes are silent, this court can look to parallel federal 
law for guidance.”).  We have also said that where a Hawaiʻ i 
civil procedure rule is identical to the federal  rule, federal  
court  interpretations of the rule  can be highly persuasive.   
Carvalho  v.  AIG  Hawai'i  Ins.  Co.,  150 Hawaiʻ i  381, 386, 502 P.3d 
482, 487 (2022).

 Several jurisdictions characterize the complete prohibition 
of a deposition as requiring “extraordinary circumstances .”  See  
Salter v. Upjohn Co., 593 F.2d 649, 651 (5th Cir. 1979) 
(observing that an order completely prohibiting a deposition is 
“very unusual” and, absent extraordinary circumstances, would 
likely constitute error); Dunford v. Rolly Marine Serv. Co., 233 
F.R.D. 635, 636 (S.D. Fla. 2005) (recognizing that protective 
orders prohibiting depositions are “rarely granted absent 
extraordinary circumstances” and require a heavy showing of good 
cause); In re McCorhill Publ’g, Inc. , 91 B.R. 223, 225 (Bankr. 
S.D.N.Y. 1988) (explaining that prohibiting an oral deposition is “a very unusual procedure” and that the movant bears a heavy 
burden of demonstrating good cause).   
Some courts have found extraordinary circumstances where 
the proposed deposition posed a substantial risk of life-
threatening physical harm. See  Dunford, 233 F.R.D. at  636 
(granting a protective order where a treating neurologist 
attested that the witness suffered from a potentially life-
threatening brain disorder); In re McCorhill  Publ’g, Inc., 91 
B.R. at  225 (granting a protective order where a treating 
physician testified that an oral deposition could threaten the 
witness’s life because of his medical condition).  
Courts have recognized that sufficiently supported 
psychological harm may likewise justify prohibiting a 
67  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
 deposition. See  Campos v. Webb Cnty., 288 F.R.D. 134, 135-36 
(S.D. Tex. 2012) (recognizing that competent medical evidence of 
severe psychological harm may warrant prohibiting a deposition, 
while concluding that the plaintiff had not yet made the 
requisite showing).  
But courts have required that a request to prohibit a 
deposition be supported by competent, specific, and documented 
medical evidence demonstrating that the proposed deposition 
poses a substantial risk of serious harm. See  Anderson v. 
Cryovac, Inc., 805 F.2d 1, 7 (1st Cir. 1986) (holding that good cause must rest on “a particular factual demonstration of 
potential harm”);  Medlin v. Andrew, 113 F.R.D. 650, 653 
(M.D.N.C. 1987) (requiring a “specific and documented factual 
showing” that a deposition would be dangerous to the deponent's 
health); Schorr v. Briarwood Estates Ltd. P’ship , 178 F.R.D. 
488, 492 (N.D. Ohio 1998) (finding good cause where the plaintiff supported the request with specific documentation from a PTSD expert); Campos, 288 F.R.D. at 136-38 (requiring detailed 
medical evidence explaining how and why the deposition would 
cause the predicted harm); Estate of Chen v. Lingting Ye, 208 
A.3d 1168, 1176-77 (R.I. 2019) (holding that conclusory 
predictions of harm are insufficient absent a particularized 
factual showing).

  Cases also hold that where such a showing has been made, 
trial courts should ordinarily consider whether reasonable 
accommodations adequately mitigate the identified risk before 
determining whether further examination should proceed. See 
Schorr, 178 F.R.D. at 492 (permitting the deposition subject to 
extensive conditions governing its manner and scope); Estate of 
Chen, 208 A.3d at 1178 -79 (recognizing the trial court’s 
authority to impose reasonable restrictions on depositions); 
Arcaris v. Superior Court , 160 Ariz. 533 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1989) 
(approving protective measures designed to facilitate accurate 
testimony while minimizing harm to a child witness); Ceron v. 
Belilovsky , 92 A.D.3d 714, 715 (2012) (upholding a protective 
order prohibiting an oral deposition where expert evidence 
established that testifying under oath would be detrimental to 
the child’s health, while permitting written interrogatories 
instead). 
Accordingly, we hold that, in general, a party seeking to 
prohibit a deposition on medical or psychological grounds must 
present competent, specific, and documented evidence 
demonstrating that the proposed deposition poses a substantial 
risk of serious harm. Generalized concerns, conclusory med ical 
opinions, or speculative predictions of future injury are 
insufficient. Upon such a showing, trial court s should consider 
whether reasonable accommodations would adequately mitigate the

   identified risk while preserving meaningful discovery. If the 
court determines that appropriate accommodations will 
sufficiently reduce the risk of harm, it may permit the 
deposition subject to those conditions. If, however, the court 
concludes that the demonstrated risk cannot be adequately 
mitigated through reasonable accommodations, it may limit or 
prohibit further examination in the exercise of its discretion 
under HRCP Rule 26.

 2.  The circuit court did not abuse its discretion by 
denying the State’s request for a HRCP Rule 35 
examination.  
The State also alleges that the circuit court abused its 
discretion by denying its request to conduct a HRCP Rule 35 
examination of Amy. 
HRCP Rule 35(a) provides , in relevant part, “[w]hen the 
mental or physical condition . . . of a party . . . is in 
controversy, the court . . . may order the party to submit to a 
physical or mental examination.” HRCP Rule 35(a). The rule 
further provides that such an order “may be made only on mot ion 
for good cause shown.” HRCP Rule 35(a). The ICA has recognized 
that a Rule 35 motion must “adequately demonstrate the existence 
of the Rule’s requirements of ‘in controversy’ and ‘good 
cause,’” and that “[t]he movant must produce sufficient 
informati on, by whatever means, so that the [trial] judge can 
fulfill [the] function mandated by the Rule.” Glover v. Grace 
Pac. Corp., 86 Hawai‘i 154, 160, 948 P.2d 575, 581 (App. 1997) 
(citation omitted). 
HRCP Rule 35 is almost identical to Rule 35 of the Federal 
Rules of Civil Procedure.   In Schlagenhauf v. Holder, 379 U.S. 
104, 118 (1964), the U.S. Supreme Court explained that the “in 
controversy” and “good cause” requirements are “plainly 
expressed limitations on the use of the Rule” and “are not met 
by mere conclusory allegations of the pleadings, nor by mere 
73  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
  
 
  
 
 
 
 relevance to the case.” Instead, each “require[s] an 
affirmative showing by the movant.” Id. 
Here, Amy’s psychological condition was clearly in 
controversy. And there was good cause for a HRCP Rule 35 
examination. 
But satisfaction of HRCP Rule 35’s threshold requirements 
does not end the inquiry. Once the moving party establishes the 
“in controversy” and “good cause” requirements , HRCP Rule 35 
provides only that the court may order the requested 
examination. HRCP Rule 35(a). The rule therefore authorizes , 
but does not require, an examination. 
Here, the circuit court did not abuse its discretion by 
denying the HRCP Rule 35 examination . By the time the State 
sought the examination, the court had spent years attempting to 
address issues regarding Amy’s continued deposition, as 
discussed above. The circuit court was well aware of 
significant concerns regarding the effect of litigation -related 
questioning on Amy’s mental health. 
Most significantly, Dr. Higa -Rogers, the court -appointed 
neutral medical expert, also recommended that the HRCP Rule 35 
exam not proceed due to the risks to Amy’s health. Dr. Higa -
Rogers opined that “the benefits of pursuing further 
questioning/testing do not appear to outweigh the potential 
74  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
  
 
 
  
 
 
  
 
 
 risks to [Amy]’s mental health, limited coping, and is unlikely 
to yield significant additional information.” 
The circuit court denied the requested examination after 
receiving this information, concluding that it posed a 
“significant risk to [Amy]’s mental health.” The circuit court 
did not err by concluding that the anticipated benefits of a 
HRCP Rule 35 examination did not justify the risks identified by 
the medical evidence. It therefore did not abuse its discretion 
by denying the HRCP Rule 35 examination requested by the State. 
Moreover, the denial of the HRCP Rule 35 examination did 
not result in substantial prejudice to the State . 
First, the State was not deprived of information concerning 
Amy’s mental condition . The State had Amy’s extensive medical 
records and reports. Second, the State does not specify what 
type of otherwise unavailable information a HRCP Rule 35 
examination was likely to produce . Finally, the State has not 
demonstrated that denial of the HRCP Rule 35 examination 
substantially prejudiced its defense. The State had completed 
two depositions of Amy before seeking the examination. In 
addition , the medical evidence consistently established that 
Amy’s severe intellectual disability rendered her highly 
suggestible and that any additional information obtained through 
a HRCP Rule 35 examination might also not be reliable.

 Under these circumstances, the circuit court did not abuse 
its discretion by denying the State’s request for a HRCP Rule 35 
examination. 
3. The damages award was not excessive . 
Finally, t he State ask s this court to reverse the circuit 
court's damages award, contending that the amount was grossly 
excessive. 
Appellate review of a damages award is highly deferential 
because the assessment of damages lies primarily within the 
province of the factfinder. See Kang, 59 Haw. at 663, 587 P.2d 
at 292. A damages award will not be disturbed unless it is 
“palpably not supported by the evidence” or is “so excessive and 
outrageous” as to demonstrate that the factfinder acted against 
the rules of law or was misled by passio n or prejudice. Kekona, 
135 Hawaiʻi at 257, 349 P.3d at 364. In a jury -waived case, the 
inquiry is limited to whether, “upon the evidence adduced, 
reasonable [people] could have come to the same conclusion as 
the . . . trial court.” Kang, 59 Haw. at 663, 587 P.2d at 292 
(citation omitted). 
Here, the record contains substantial evidence supporting 
the award. The court conducted a sixty -six-day bench trial, 
heard testimony from more than thirty witnesses, and made 
extensive findings regarding both liability and damages. The 
record amply supp orts the damages awar ds.

 Thus, the damages award ed are not excessive.
