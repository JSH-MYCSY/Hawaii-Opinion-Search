 Why not? It has not met.  So it cannot select a 
chairperson.  Some of its members tried to bring the Commission 
together, but the way was never clear to them. 
This is no minor board. In 2004, the legislature created 
the Elections Commission to oversee the Office of Elections 
(Office), replacing an earlier review panel with a standing 
commission. 
It appoints the chief election officer who heads 
the Office and may remove that officer for cause. Hawaiʻi 
Revised Statutes (HRS) §§ 11-1.6 (2009 & Supp. 2026), 11-7.5

the Office.  It relays messages between the members.

roster of people who have asked to be notified of its meetings. 
The Sunshine Law requires notice to them before any meeting. 
HRS § 92-7 (Supp. 2024). 
To come together at all, the Commission depends on the very 
Office it supervises.

HRS § 11-1.5(b) (2009).  
The Office refused. It owed no support, it maintained,

A.  Standing  
The Office challenges Cushnie and McKay’s standing. 
Petitioners have it. 
Standing in Hawaiʻ i “is a prudential concern and not an 
issue of subject matter jurisdiction.” Tax Found. of Haw. v. 
State, 144 Hawaiʻ i 175, 190, 439 P.3d 127, 142 (2019). The 
“touchstone remains the needs of justice.”  Life of the Land v. 
Land Use Comm’n of State of Haw., 63 Haw. 166, 176, 623 P.2d 
431, 441 (1981) (cleaned up). 
To establish standing, Petitioners must allege a personal 
stake in the outcome. See id. at 172, 623 P.2d at 438. The 
stake must warrant invocation of the court’s jurisdiction and justify exercise of its remedial powers. Id. 
Cushnie and McKay have one. As duly appointed Elections 
Commissioners, they have a direct stake in whether the Office of

Commission needs. Without support, the Commission’s work goes 
undone. 
No staff support means no one to notice a meeting.  No one 
to publish an agenda.  No one to distribute instructions for 
public testimony. See HRS § 92-7(a). Without those statutory 
steps, a Commission meeting violates the Sunshine Law. 
If the Commission can’t meet, the commissioners cannot 
fulfill their statutory duties. See, e.g., HRS § 11-7.5 (the 
Elections Commission shall “[h]old public hearings,” “[e]mploy . . . a full-time chief election officer,” and 
“[c]onduct a performance evaluation of the chief election 
officer within two months after the date a general election is 
certified”). 
That’s a personal stake. Commissioners are bound by law to 
carry out those statutory responsibilities. See id. And it is 
the Commission, not the Office, that answers for Sunshine Law 
compliance. See HRS §§ 92-11 (2012), 92-12(c) (Supp. 2024).  
According to the Office, Petitioners lack standing without 
prior Commission approval. In its view, neither § 11-7 nor 
§ 11-7.5 allows individual commissioners to initiate actions 
absent a majority vote. 
We disagree.

1. Sunshine Law 
The Sunshine Law governs how the Commission meets. And it 
shapes what the commissioners may do beforehand. 
The Sunshine Law’s command is openness, start to finish. 
Every meeting of a board is open to the public. HRS § 92-3. 
Boards must honor the law’s spirit and purpose at all times. 
Kanahele v. Maui County Council, 130 Hawaiʻ i 228, 248, 307 P.3d 
1174, 1194 (2013). 
The openness depends on a broad meaning of what a meeting 
is. HRS § 92-2 defines a “meeting” as “the convening of a board 
for which a quorum is required in order to make a decision or to 
deliberate toward a decision” on a matter within the board’s 
authority.  That quorum is a majority of the Commission members   
–  five, even with the chair’s seat vacant. HRS § 92-15.  The  
definition extends past formal deliberation to discussion.  Two 
members may “discuss between themselves matters relating to 
board business” only while they remain fewer than a quorum and 
seek no commitment to vote. HRS § 92-2.5(a) (Supp. 2024).

2. Whose Power to Convene 
Who may call a meeting of the Commission and issue notice? The Office says the chairperson or a majority of the 
members. Petitioners say a single member. The Sunshine Law has nothing to say. The question involves governance under chapter 
11 and the Commission’s rules. 
An administrative body holds only the powers the 
legislature delegated. It has no general or common-law

 authority to supply what the statutes omit. Preble v. Bd. of 
Trs. of Emps.’ Ret. Sys. of State of Haw., 111 Hawaiʻ i 498, 505, 
143 P.3d 37, 44 (2006). 
Two statutory provisions control the Commission’s voting 
and internal operation.  HRS § 11-7(d) entrusts the Commission’s 
business to a majority vote of its membership and lets it set 
its own procedures. HRS § 11-7(a)(5) and (b) direct the 
Commission to select its chairperson by a two-thirds vote. 
Chapter 11 says nothing about how the Commission convenes. 
See HRS §§ 11-7, 11-7.5.  The administrative rules answer it. 
They assign those functions to the body.  HAR § 3-170-14 
provides that “[t]he elections commission shall give written public notice,” “file the notice,” “maintain a list of names and 
addresses,” and “not change the agenda, once filed,” without a 
two-thirds recorded vote. At every turn the rules make the 
Commission the actor. 
On February 4, 2026, the Commission voted unanimously to 
designate Robert’s Rules of Order as its parliamentary 
authority. Meetings were then held under those rules until the Commission’s last meeting on June 10, 2026. 
The Office now protests. Robert’s Rules amounts to 
unlawful rulemaking, it charges, thus the Commission may not 
elect a pro tem chair to run a meeting.

     Not so. Under HRS § 11-7(d), the Commission “shall  
establish its own procedures, except as may be provided by law.”   
How it runs its meetings and conducts orderly business is one of 
those procedures.   This internal decision is exempt from the 
Hawaiʻi Administrative Procedure Act’s (HAPA) rulemaking 
requirement.  
HAPA’s rulemaking does not touch a regulation “concerning 
only the internal management of an agency and not affecting 
private rights of or procedures available to the public.” HRS 
§ 91-1 (Supp. 2017). A rule, by contrast, prescribes agency 
procedure of general or particular application and affects the 
public’s rights in the abstract. Green Party of Haw. v. Nago, 
138 Hawaiʻ i 228, 237, 378 P.3d 944, 953 (2016); see Souza v. 
Nago, No. SCEC-26-0000598, 2026 WL 2654175, at *6 (Haw. Sep. 4, 2026), as amended (Sep. 11, 2026) (separate opinion of Eddins, 
J.) (method for breaking an election tie — names sealed in 
plastic eggs, dropped in a lau hala basket, shaken, and drawn — 
is a rule, because it established the agency’s procedure for 
future ties and affected candidates and voters). 
Robert’s Rules orders the Commission’s own meetings and 
affects no one else. 
Ordinarily, the chairperson calls the Commission’s 
meetings. Nothing here disturbs that practice.

do so.  Under the Commission’s rules, the chairperson has three

11(c), and designating a member to preside as chair for a

Reading HRS § 11-7(d) and HAR § 3-170-14 together, the 
Commission holds the power to convene. When the chair’s seat sits vacant, a member may exercise that power. 
The Commission has no vice-chairperson.  With the chair’s 
seat open, Robert’s Rules takes it from there. A member calls 
the meeting to order. The members then elect a chairperson pro 
tem. § 47:11(3).  A majority vote binds the Commission. HRS 
§ 92-15; HRS § 11-7(d).  
The Commission did this long before it adopted Robert’s 
Rules. From 2004 through 2007, with no chairperson selected, it 
convened and organized itself. 
The statute’s architecture empowers the Elections 
Commission. Act 57 created the Commission to oversee the Office of Elections and gave it real control over that Office - the 
authority to appoint and remove the Chief Election Officer who 
runs it, and evaluate performance after each general election.

3. A Single Member May Set the Meeting in Motion 
In the Office’s view, a chairperson calls a meeting alone, 
no vote needed.  Without a chairperson, it takes a five-person 
majority. Petitioners say one is enough. 
The members cannot notice a meeting on their own. The 
Office holds the list and assists only on a chairperson’s 
request or a Commission vote. A chairless commission that has 
not met is locked out of both – no chair to ask, no meeting for 
the vote.  The meeting needs the Office, and the Office waits 
for the meeting. A catch-22. 
The statute breaks the circle. HRS § 11-1.5(b) directs the 
Office to provide the Commission the staff support it requests. A request by the Commission activates the duty.  The statute 
requires no prior vote. 
When the chair seat is empty and the schedule stalls, a 
single member’s request to convene is a request by the 
Commission. The Office must provide the support. Second-
guessing the member oversteps its role.

to the other commissioners. And sets the Office’s scheduling

Scheduling is not board business. So a commissioner may 
communicate with all members to learn when a quorum is free. 
See HRS § 92-5(b) (2012 & Supp. 2022). Finding a date is logistics. The Commission’s business waits for the public 
meeting. 
The agenda is different. Without a chair, the first 
meeting’s business is organizational – electing a chairperson 
pro tem. The notice must say so. Once filed, the agenda may 
not be enlarged except by a two-thirds recorded vote and not at 
all to add an item of reasonably major importance affecting a 
significant number of people. HRS § 92-7(a), (d); HAR § 3-170-
14(a), (c). Future agendas come from the chair or commission vote.

argument goes like this. Petitioners invoke § 47:11(3).  But 
that provision governs who calls a meeting to order once the

The argument has a practical side.  Five are needed for a

  4. Electing a Chairperson Pro Tem 
One question remains.  Resolving it now spares the parties 
a second trip to this court. When the commissioners convene, 
their first item of business is electing a chairperson pro tem.  
The split from June 10 could return. Five votes or six? 
The question is properly before us. Petitioners disclaim 
any request that we resolve governance matters.  But the 
Office’s defense turns on a governance premise.  Only a selected chairperson, or a Commission vote, it contends, may call a 
meeting. 
The defense pulls in what the petition tried to leave out. 
Civil Beat held that a board’s misunderstanding of the Sunshine 
Law was a live controversy fit for decision. Members who 
“misconstrue the Sunshine Law and take action based on these 
misconceptions” undermine its intent and the public’s “right to 
know.” 144 Hawaiʻ i 466, 476–77, 445 P.3d 47, 57–58 (2019); HRS 
§ 92-1 (2012). A squabble that has already halted a commission 
with duties to perform is a live problem of the same order. See 
HRS § 11-7.5(5). 
The text settles it. 
The two-thirds requirement of HRS 
§ 11-7(b) applies to select “[t]he chairperson of the elections 
commission under subsection (a)(5).” That person is the ninth 
member, selected by the eight appointed under subsections (a)(1) 
through (4).

D. Why the Writ Issues Mandamus is an extraordinary remedy. It issues only where 
the petitioner shows a clear and indisputable right to the relief requested, and no other means to adequately redress the 
wrong. Womble Bond Dickinson (US) LLP v. Kim, 153 Hawaiʻ i 307, 
319, 537 P.3d 1154, 1166 (2023); see Miller v. Collins, 158 Hawaiʻi 435, 439, 593 P.3d 374, 378 (2026). To compel a public 
officer’s duty, the duty must also be ministerial. Barnett v. 
Broderick, 84 Hawaiʻ i 109, 111, 929 P.2d 1359, 1361 (1996). 
Petitioners satisfy each condition. 
The duty is ministerial. HRS § 11-1.5(b) commands that the 
Office “shall provide staff support . . . as requested by the 
elections commission.” The verb is mandatory.  The statute 
spells out who must act, what they must do, and what sets the 
duty off, with the precision of a ministerial act. See Salling 
v. Moon, 76 Hawaiʻ i 273, 274 n.3, 874 P.2d 1098, 1099 n.3 (1994). 
Nothing is left to discretion. 
No adequate alternative remedy was available. A natural 
candidate is the Sunshine Law’s own enforcement action. HRS 
§ 92-12(c).  But that action is brought against a board for a 
prohibited act, and Petitioners allege none.  The Office of 
Elections is not a board, and, as OIP advised, the Sunshine Law

 does not directly apply to it. A suit under § 92-12(c) could do 
nothing to let the Commission meet. 
A declaratory action comes closer. HRS § 632-1 (2016) was 
theoretically open. See Alakaʻ i Na Keiki, Inc. v. Matayoshi, 127 
Hawaiʻi 263, 284, 277 P.3d 988, 1009 (2012). But a suit over so 
narrow a matter would mean months more of litigation, if not longer – on top of the months this case has already run.  Petitioners want a prompt, lawful meeting. 
For commissioners 
who seek only to convene their own board lawfully, a declaratory action is too slow and too roundabout a remedy. Mandamus within

The right to relief is clear and indisputable. HRS § 11

Cushnie and McKay asked the Office to notice a meeting and

Petitioners’ right to the Office’s performance is settled. 
The Office’s view cannot unsettle it. A duty is clear, 
cramped reading or not. Otherwise, a public officer could

IV. 
The petition for a writ of mandamus is granted. 
The Elections Commission can meet. The law sees to it. 
When the Commission is chairless, a single member may ask 
the Office of Elections for staff support.  The Office must 
provide it. 
When there is no chairperson, on the request of any member, 
the Office of Elections shall give the staff support the 
Commission needs to notice and hold a meeting.  The Commission 
may then convene and take up electing a chairperson pro tem. 
The Sunshine Law promises an open government. Its purpose 
is to protect “the people’s right to know.” HRS § 92-1(1).  
That promise begins with a board able to meet. The Commission may now do so, and when it does, the public will be watching.
