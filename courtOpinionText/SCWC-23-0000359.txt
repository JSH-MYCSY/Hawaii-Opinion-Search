IV. Discussion 
A. The circuit court did not consider sexual penetration or 
other improper factors , including the alleged lack of 
remorse, and therefore did not abuse its discretion by 
denying the DANC motion . 
1. HRS Chapter 853 Deferrals 
HRS chapter 853, which allows for and governs DAGs and 
DANCs, serves important policy goals:  
[I]n certain criminal cases, particularly those involving 
first time, accidental, or situational offenders, it is in 
the best interest of the State and the defendant that the 
defendant be given the opportunity to keep [the 
defendant ’s] record free of a criminal conviction if [the 
defendant] can comply with certain terms and conditions 
during a period designated by court order. Especially 
where youth is involved, a record free of a felony 
conviction, which would foreclose certain educational, 
professional, and job opportunities may, in a proper case, 
be more conducive to offender rehabilitation and crime 
prevention than the deter rent effects of a conviction and 
sentence. 
1976 Haw. Sess. Laws Act 154, § 1 at 279. 
For eligible defendants and offenses, a  trial court may 
defer acceptance of a  defendant’s guilty or no contest plea and 
impose conditions of deferral, provided that:  
(1) [The] defendant voluntarily pleads guilty or nolo 
contendere, prior to commencement of trial, to a 
felony, misdemeanor, or petty misdemeanor; 
(2) It appears to the court that the defendant is not 
likely again to engage in a criminal course of conduct; 
and 
15  **FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ‘I REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER** 
  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 (3) The ends of justice and the welfare of society do not 
require that the defendant shall presently suffer the 
penalty imposed by law , 
the court, without accepting the plea of nolo contendere or 
entering a judgment of guilt and with the consent of the 
defendant and after considering the recommendations, if 
any, of the prosecutor, may defer further proceedings. 
HRS § 853 -1(a). 
If a trial court decides to grant a deferral, it has 
discretion within parameters to  determine the period of time for 
which proceedings should  be deferred and what conditions to 
impose. Then, if a defendant satisfies the specified conditions 
for the deferral  period, the defendant avoids a criminal 
conviction, the charge(s) are  dismissed, and the defendant’s 
corresponding arrest record can be expunged:  
(b) The proceedings may be deferred upon any of the 
conditions specified by section 706 -624 . . . The court 
may defer the proceedings for a period of time as the 
court shall direct but in no case to exceed the maximum 
sentence allowable; provided that, if the defendant has 
entered a plea of guilty or nolo contendere to a petty 
misdemeanor, the court ma y defer the proceedings for a 
period not to exceed one year [.] 
(c) Upon the defendant ’s completion of the period 
designated by the court and in compliance with the terms 
and conditions established, the court shall discharge 
the defendant and dismiss the charge against the 
defendant. 
(d) Discharge of the defendant and dismissal of the charge 
against the defendant under this section shall be 
without adjudication of guilt, shall eliminate any civil 
admission of guilt, and is not a conviction. 
(e) Upon discharge of the defendant and dismissal of the 
charge against the defendant under this section, the 
defendant may apply for expungement not less than one 
year following discharge, pursuant to section 831 -3.2. 
HRS § 853 -1(b)-(e).

  But HRS § 853-4 excludes some offenses  from deferral 
eligibility. Relevant to this case, HRS § 853-4(a)(13)(O) and 
(P) expressly disallow DAGs and DANCs for sexual assault in the 
second and third degrees. But sexual assault in the fourth 
degree  is DAG/DANC eligible. Therefore, Gaeta’s original 
charges were not eligible for deferral, while Count 4,  to which 
he pled,  is a deferral eligible offense.  
Also, generally, “sentencing cases are persuasive, though 
not controlling authority, in the [DANC] context.” Satoafaiga , 
150 Hawai‘i at 418 n.17, 504 P.3d at 3 36 n.17. And under HRS § 
706-606, sentencing courts are to consider “ [t]he nature and 
circumstances of the offense and the history and characteristics 
of the defendant[.]” “ [T]he nature and circumstances” factor 
applies not just to sentencing of convicted defendants, but also 
to DANC pleas under HRS § 853 -1(a)(2)-(3). Satoafaiga , 150 
Hawai‘i at 421-22, 504 P.3d at 3 39-40. This court stated : 
In the [DANC] context, a court cannot intelligently decide 
whether a defendant is likely to reoffend nor whether their crimes merit immediate punishment  without understanding the 
nature of the offense and the defendant ’s character and 
circumstances. Thus, judges not only may but must consider 
the defendant’s particular situation  in DANC proceedings as 
well as in sentencing.  
Id. (Emphases added .) 
Further, judges have broad discretion in deciding whether 
to grant DANC motions. Satoafaiga , 150 Hawai‘i at 421, 504 P.3d 
at 339. An abuse of discretion occurs only “if the trial court

 has clearly exceeded the bounds of reason or has disregarded 
rules or principles of law or practice to the substantial 
detriment of a party litigant.” Davia, 87 Hawai‘i at 253, 953 
P.2d at 1351 (cleaned up ). 
As noted, HRS § 853 -1(a) requires satisfaction of 
subsections (1) through (3) for a deferral to be granted . As a 
preliminary matter, the State and Gaeta agree that Gaeta 
satisfied the first two. Gaeta voluntarily pled no contest 
before commencement of trial. The circuit court also did not 
believe Gaeta posed a danger of recidivism; possible recidivism 
was not a factor in the DANC denial. The circuit court stated, 
“No question he’s . . . qualified to get the deferral.” 
Therefore , as the parties agree, the only issue is whether 
the circuit court abused its discretion in its determination of 
the final condition, whether “[t]he ends of justice and the 
welfare of society do not require that the defendant shall 
presently suffer the penalty imposed by law[.]” HRS § 853 -
1(a)(3). Gaeta argues that the circuit court abused its 
discretion because its conclusion on the third factor was based 
on improper consideration of sexual penetration and remorse. 
We disagree and hold that the circuit court considered 
neither sexual penetration, remorse , nor other improper factors .

  2. The circuit court did not improperly consider sexual

In the sentencing context, we have explicitly held that the 
“impact on the victim” can be considered as part of the 
circumstances of the offense. See  State v. Hussein, 122 Hawai‘i 
495,  510, 229 P.3d 313,  328 (2010) (ruling that in deciding

the dismissed charges of sexual penetration.

3. The circuit court also did not improperly consider 
Gaeta’s lack of remorse. 
Gaeta also argues the circuit court improperly considered 
lack of remorse. He focuses on the circuit court’s earl ier

remarked that Gaeta appeared to lack remorse for his conduct,

considering lack of remorse in denying the DANC motion. The 
circuit court clearly stated that it based its DANC denial on 
the “impact on the victim ,” which was properly considered , as 
discussed a bove. 
Therefore, the circuit court did not improperly consider 
lack of remorse in denying the DANC motion. 
We therefore reject Gaeta’s issues on certiorari.

We now address the two questions for which we ordered

   1.  A plea agreement provision that attempts to override 
judicial precedent prohibiting consideration of 
dismissed charges seeks imposition of an illegal 
sentence and violates a defendant’s rights under 
Article I, Sections 5 and 14 of the Hawaiʻi 
Constitution and must not be included in plea 
agreements.  
The first question for which we ordered supplemental 
briefing relates to a provision in Gaeta’s second plea agreement 
that the “[f]acts and circumstances of dismissed charges may be 
considered at sentencing .” The provision was apparently 
inserted by the State to allow the circuit court to consider the 
dismissed s exual penetration charges, in direct contravention of 
this court’s precedent discussed below . 
Here, the circuit court clearly said it was not considering 
the dismissed sexual penetration counts , and the State never 
argued this plea agreement provision as a basis for affirming 
the circuit court’s decision. But we ordered supplemental 
briefing based on the State’s apparent attempt to circumvent 
judicial precedent designed to protect a criminal defendant’s 
constitutional rights . 
Our case law has consistently held that a trial court’s 
consideration of dismissed or acquitted charges in sentencing 
violates a defendant’s constitutional rights.   See  Satoafaiga, 
150 Hawaiʻi at 418, 504 P.3d at 336; State v. Vellina, 106 
Hawaiʻi 441, 450, 106 P.3d 364, 373 (2005); State v. Nunes, 72 
Haw. 521, 525, 824 P.2d 837, 840 (1992). Satoafaiga  explained 
22  **FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ‘I REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER** 
    
 
    that punishing a defendant’s dismissed charges of sexual 
penetration was analogous to punishing uncharged crimes, 
“[raising] serious constitutional questions” and implicating a 
defendant’s Hawai ʻi constitutional rights under Article I, 
Section 5 (due process) and 14 (to be informed of the nature and 
cause of the accusation ). 150 Hawai ʻi at 418, 504 P.3d at 336 
(citing Nunes, 72 Haw. at 525, 824 P.2d at 840). 
We have also recognized that plea agreements implicate a 
defendant’s Article I, Section 5 due process rights. State v. 
Adams, 76 Hawaiʻi 408, 412, 879 P.2d 513, 517 (1994) . 
In modern times, plea agreements abound. Criminal justice 
today is for the most part a system of pleas, not a system of 
trials. Lafler v. Cooper, 566 U.S. 156, 170 (2012).   In Hawaiʻi, 
in the fiscal year ending June 30, 2025, less than 200 Hawaiʻi 
circuit court criminal cases were resolved through trial while 
almost 2,000 were resolved through pleas.  The Judiciary State 
of Hawai‘ i, 2025 Annual Caseload Report  9 (2026), 
https://www.courts.state.hi.us/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/FY2025_annual_report_statistical_supplem 
ent_Final_Draft-2.pdf [https://perma.cc/APA4-J9UH].   Although 
precise statistics are not readily available, an overwhelming 
majority of these circuit court pleas probably involved plea 
agreements.  
In Adams, this court said this regarding plea agreements :

   A plea agreement is essentially a contract entered into 
between the State and the defendant, in which the defendant 
agrees to plead guilty or no contest to a charge and to 
forego certain constitutional rights (including the right 
to trial) in exchange for which the State promises some 
form of leniency or cooperation in prosecutions. 
76 Hawaiʻi at 412, 879 P.2d at 517. 
But plea agreements are not commercial contracts . They 
implicate the enforcement and waiver of critical constitutional 
rights. And even when analogizing commercial contract 
principles, plea agreements are in the nature of adhesion 
contracts, as the government has much greater bargaining power. 
Therefore, resorting to contract principles cannot determin e 
rights and duties under a plea agreement . Id. 
Granted, in a plea agreement, a defendant may waive 
important pretrial and trial constitutional rights , such as the 
right to a jury trial or right to testify. See, e.g., State v. 
Gomez-Lobato, 130 Hawai‘i 465, 469, 312 P.3d 897 , 901 (2013) 
(involving waiving the right to a jury trial); Tachibana v. 
State, 79 Hawaiʻi 226, 232, 900 P.2d 1293, 1299 (1995) (involving 
waiving the right to testify). But there are plea provisions 
that implicate the fairness of the plea . Adams, 76 Hawaiʻi at 
412, 879 P.2d at 517. 
And  as a sentence is determined after  a plea is accepted, 
absent an agreement regarding sentencing  that the court has 
agreed to bind itself to under Hawaiʻi Rules of Penal Procedure 
(“HRPP”) Rule 11(f)(1)  (eff. 2014),  a defendant does not  know

   what sentence will be imposed . Therefore, a plea of no contest 
or guilty does not constitute a waiver of an appeal of the 
sentence on the grounds that it is illegal. State v. Dudoit , 90 
Hawaiʻi 262, 265 n.2, 978 P.2d 700 , 703 n.2 (1999). In other 
words, plea agreements cannot seek a waiver of the imposition of 
an illegal sentence. 
This non -waiver of an illegal sentence is also recognized 
in HRPP Rule 40(a)(1)(iii) (eff. 2006), which allows a defendant 
to seek relief from an illegal sentence even after an 
unsuccessful direct appeal. 
Fundamentally , we interpret the Hawaiʻi Constitution on its 
own terms , and we provide greater protection of a criminal 
defendant’s due process rights than the United States Supreme 
Court does under the federal Due Process Clause. Granillo v. 
State, No. SCWC-22-0000740, 2026 WL 2085397, at *27 (Hawai ʻi July 
15, 2026). Hawaiʻi’s Due Process Clause respects human dignity. 
State v. Tolentino , 158 Hawaiʻi 321, 334, 592 P.3d 209, 222 
(2026). 
Hence, w e hold that a plea agreement provision that 
attempts to override judicial precedent prohibiting 
consideration of dismissed charges in sentencing seeks 
imposition of an illegal sentence and therefore violates a 
defendant’s rights under Article I, Sections 5 and 14. 
Therefore, p lea agreement s must not include such provision s.

 2. Courts cannot consider l ack of r emorse in the context 
of no contest pleas . 
As we ruled earlier, the circuit court did not improperly 
consider remorse in denying Gaeta’s DANC motion and sentencing 
him to probation. Yet, in the context of this case involving a 
no contest plea, the ICA explicitly stated that “a court 
generally can consider a defendant’s lack of remorse in the 
sentencing context ,” citing Satoafaiga , 150 Hawai‘i at 421, 504 
P.3d at 339 . Gaeta, 2025 WL 2466115 , at *3. But our law on 
whether a lack of remorse can be considered in the context of a 
no contest plea is unclear . We therefore also ordered 
supplemental briefing on th is issue. 
Federal and state courts, including the ICA, have generally 
held that a lack of remorse can be considered. See, e.g., 
United States  v. Harris, 751 F.3d 123, 127  (3d Cir. 2014); State 
v. Knight, 701 N.W.2d 83, 87-88  (Iowa 2005); see  also  State v. 
Kaluna, No. CAAP-23-0000096, 2024 WL 637400, at *4 (Haw. App. 
Feb. 15, 2024) (SDO)  (holding that the defendant’s lack of 
remorse for the psychological harm suffered by a victim of a 
kidnapping charge to which the defendant pled no contest was a 
characteristic of the defendant that could properly be 
considered).  
And we have previously ruled that a trial court abused its 
discretion by failing to consider the defendant’s expressed

 remorse before rejecting his DANC motion after a no contest 
plea. State v. Medeiros , 146 Hawai ‘i 1, 11, 454 P.3d 1069, 1079 
(2019). Therefore, a defendant can choose to express remorse 
after a no contest plea , although such expressions could be used 
adversely in a civil proceeding arising out of the acts 
constituting the crime . 
But the issue here is whether a court can consider a 
defendant’s failure to express remorse in the context of ruling 
on a DANC motion or sentencing. 
In Satoafaiga , we explicitly declined to address the issue 
of whether lack of remorse can be considered in the context of 
no contest pleas. We stated: 
[Defendant ]  appears to argue for a rule displacing the 
analysis in Kamanaʻ o  and categorically barring judges from 
considering a defendant’s remorse in cases involving a no 
contest plea. Because we hold that the underlying conduct 
for which Satoafaiga was asked to apologize was improperly 
considered, we do not address whether Kamanaʻo  properly 
applies in the context of a no contest plea.  
150 Hawaiʻi at 421 n.23, 504 P.3d at 339 n.23 . 
State v.  Kamanaʻo, 103 Hawai ʻi 315, 82 P.3d 401  (2003), 
cited in this passage from Satoafaiga, was not a plea case; it 
involved a defendant’s refusal to admit guilt with respect to  an  
offense for which a jury  found him guilty. 103 Hawai ʻi at 316, 
82 P.3d at 402. We held that, although a sentencing court may 
consider a defendant’s lack of remorse in evaluating the 
likelihood of rehabilitation, it was  prohibited from imposing an

even after the jury conviction. 103 Hawai ʻi at 324, 82 P.3d at 
410. 
This court has said that “[t]he principle difference 
between a plea of guilty and a plea of nolo contendere is that 
the latter may not be used against the defendant in a civil 
action based upon the same acts .” State v. Nguyen , 81 Hawaiʻi 
279, 281 n.1, 916 P.2d 689, 691 n.1 (1996) . But there are two 
additional , salient difference s between guilty and no contest 
pleas, which are reflected in HRPP Rule s 11(b) and (g): 
(b) 
 . . . . No contest. A defendant may plead no contest only with 
the consent of the court. Such a plea shall be accepted 
by the court only after due consideration of the views 
of the parties and the interest of the public in the 
effective administration of justice. 
(g) Determining accuracy of plea. Notwithstanding the 
acceptance of a plea of guilty, the court shall not 
enter a judgment upon such plea without making such 
inquiry as shall satisfy it that there is a factual 
basis for the plea. 
As can be seen, u nder HRPP Rule 11(b), in contrast with 
guilty pleas, a defendant can only plead no contest when a judge 
consents. Also, under HRPP Rule 11(g), in contrast with guilty 
pleas, no factual basis is required for no contest pleas. If a 
court is not satisfied that a no contest plea serve s “the 
interest of the public in the effective administration of 
justice,” the court does not have to allow a no contest plea; it

can insist on a guilty plea for a plea agreement . The defendant 
can otherwise go to trial. 
Therefore , no contest pleas are different from guilty 
pleas. If a trial court consents to allow a no contest plea, a 
criminal defendant has the right not to admit to a factual basis 
for the plea . 
Relatedly, t he Vermont Supreme Court in State v. Gleason , 
576 A.2d 1246, 1250 (Vt. 1990) , held that while a no contest 
plea effectuates a waiver of a defendant’s privilege against 
self-incrimination, the waiver is limited, and only extends “to 
the determination of guilt or innocence of the crime for which 
the plea is entered ,” and, while “awaiting sentencing, a 
defendant who has pled [no contest] m ay invoke the privilege to 
prevent the possible enhancement of the sentence. ” 
We agree with the Vermont Supreme Court. Our law already 
implicitly recognizes what was stated by that court.   HRPP Rule 
11(g) does not require a factual basis for a no contest plea. 
Nguyen  recognizes that the purpose of a no contest plea is to 
allow defendants to avoid making statements that can be used 
against them in civil proceedings. Kamanaʻo  stands for the 
proposition that a court cannot impose an enhanced sentence 
based on a defendant’s refusal to admit guilt.   
Hence, we hold that e ven after a no contest plea, a 
defendant continues to hold a limited right against self -

 incrimination based on Article I, Section 10 of the Hawaiʻi 
Constitution against the making of statements that might impact 
sentencing or the grant or denial of a DANC motion. We further 
prospectively hold that, due to the limited waiver of the right 
against self -incrimination for a no contest plea , a defendant’s 
failure to express remorse cannot be used against the defendant . 
As this is a new rule of constitutional interpretation, it 
applies to events occurring after the date of this opinion. 
We are therefore establish ing another bright -line rule that 
enforces a defendant’s right against self -incrimination under 
Article I, Section 10. As we said in State v. Hewitt , 153 
Hawaiʻi 33, 44, 526 P.3d 558, 569 (2023) : 
Bright-line rules foster uniformity and predictability.   
See  Antonin Scalia,  The  Rule  of  Law  as  a  Law  of  Rules,  56  
U.  Chi.  L.  Rev.  1175,  1179  (1989).  The most significant 
role of judges may be to protect the individual criminal 
defendant against the occasional excesses of popular will, and to preserve the checks and balances within our 
constitutional system that are designed to inhibit that 
popular will .   56  U.  Chi.  L.  Rev.  at  1180. In terms of 
constitutional rules of criminal procedure, in order to preserve checks and balances, bright -line rules are 
therefore preferable.  
This new bright line  eliminates the confusion that has 
existed in our case  law regarding whether a court can consider a 
defendant’s failure to express remorse after a no contest plea 
in determining whether to grant a DANC motion or what sentence 
to impose.
