III.  Standards of Review  
A.  Agency Appeal  
Our review of a  circuit court decision on an HRS § 91-14 
agency appeal is a secondary appeal, in which we decide whether

 the circuit court was right or wrong,  by applying the standards 
of HRS § 91-14(g) to the BVA’s decision and order.  Flores v. 
Bd. of Land & Nat. Res., 143 Hawai‘i 114, 120, 424 P.3d 469, 475 
(2018).  HRS § 91-14(g) provides:  
Upon review of the record, the court may affirm the

(1) In violation of constitutional or statutory

(3) Made upon unlawful procedure;

(6) Arbitrary, or capricious, or characterized by

B. Interpretation of Agency Rules 
The general principles of construction which apply to 
statutes also apply to administrative rules. As in 
statutory construction, courts look first at an 
administrative rule ’s language. If an administrative 
rule’s language is unambiguous, and its literal application 
is neither inconsistent with the policies of the statute 
the rule implements nor produces an absurd or unjust 
result, courts enforce the rule ’s plain meaning. 
Int’l Bhd. of Elec. Workers, Local 1357 v. Hawaiian Tel. Co., 68 
Haw. 316, 323, 713 P.2d 943, 950 (1986).  
An agency’s interpretation of its own rules is generally 
entitled to deference.  Gillan v. Gov’t Emps. Ins. Co., 119 
Hawai‘i 109, 125, 194 P.3d 1071, 1087 (2008) (“Arguably, where an 
agency promulgates a rule, we will accord consideration to its 
interpretation of its own rules.” (citation omitted)).

IV. Discussion 
A. RPP § 12 -801-60 generally instructs the BVA to file FOF/COL 
within 45 -days. 
Before we address the effect of the rule, we address its 
interpretation.  
1. The 45-day deadline actually began when the case was 
“submitted for decision” on November 24, 2021 . 
RPP § 12 -801-60 provides: 
A proceeding shall stand submitted for decision by the 
board after  the taking of evidence, the submission of a  
report by the hearing officer, and the filing of such briefs or the presentation of such oral argument as may 
have been allowed.  A party to the proceeding may submit  a 
proposed decision and order which shall include  proposed 
findings of fact.  Said proposals shall be  mailed to each 
party to the proceeding and an opportunity  given to each 
party to comment thereon.  
Every decision  and order adverse to a party to the  
proceeding, rendered by the board in a contested case,  
shall be in writing or stated in the record and shall be 
accompanied by separate findings of fact and conclusions of 
law.  If any party to the proceeding has filed proposed 
findings of fact, the board shall incorporate in  its 
decision a ruling which addresses such findings.   Such 
decisions and orders in contested cases shall be rendered 
within forty -five days from the presentation of oral 
argument by the parties.   
(Emphasis added.) 
The language in the rule is somewhat confusing , and the 
parties disagree on whether RPP § 12 -801-60’s 45-day deadline 
was triggered on September 20, 2021 , or November 24, 2021. The 
16  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
 
  
 
   
 
 
  
 
   
 
 
 
 
 
  ICA assumed the clock began running on November 24, 2021 , and 
held that the BVA in any event violated RPP § 12 -801-60. 
Norrie, 2026 WL 1266063, at * 4. Norrie argue s that this ruling 
was incorrect and that the BVA i mproperly reopened the oral 
argument on October 28, 2021 , so the oral argument on September 
20, 2021 triggered the 45 -day deadline . 
The second paragraph of RPP § 12 -801-60 says that FOF/COL 
must be entered within forty -five days of oral argument. But 
the first paragraph says that “a proceeding shall stand 
submitted for decision by the board after the taking of 
evidence, the submission of a report by the hearing officer, and 
the filing of such briefs or the presentation of such oral 
argument as may have been allowed.” RPP § 12 -801-60. And RPP § 
12-801-59 (eff. 1999) empowers the BVA to “reopen the docket and 
take further evidence or may make other disposition of the case 
that is necessary under the circumstances.” 
The rule is confusing as to when the 45 -day deadline begins 
to run. The second paragraph says the 45 -day deadline is 
triggered by oral argument. But the first paragraph says that a 
matter is not “submitted for decision” until briefing has 
concluded. As in this case, it is possible for briefing to be 
concluded after oral arguments. Depending on the complexity of 
issues before the BVA , post-hearing briefing might even be 
concluded more than forty -five days after a n oral argument. It 
17  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ʻI REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
 
  
 
 
   
 
   
   
 
 
  would be unworkable and absurd for a 45-day deadline for 
rendering FOF/COL to be triggered when briefing has not 
concluded. 
We therefore hold that the 45 -day deadline under this rule 
begins to run when the case is “submitted for decision,” in 
other words, when everything applicable to a specific hearing 
under RPP § 12-801-60 has occurred : the “taking of evidence, the 
submission of a report by the hearing officer, and the filing of 
such briefs or the presentation of such oral argument as may 
have been allowed.” 
Hence, Norrie’s argument that the deadline began on

B. In any event, RPP § 12 -801-60 is a directory , not 
mandatory , rule. 
Before the ICA, the BVA said it “does not dispute the 45 -
day deadline provided in the rule should be accorded mandatory 
effect.” But we are not bound by the BVA’s concession or 
interpretation of law. Umberger , 140 Hawaiʻi at 520 n.38, 403 
P.3d at 297 n.38. “While an agency’s interpretation of its own 
rules is generally entitled to deference, this court does not 
defer to agency interpretations that are plainly erroneous or

 inconsistent with the underlying legislative purpose.” Kaupiko 
v. Bd. of Land & Nat. Res., 154 Hawaiʻi 456, 468-69, 555 P.3d 
143, 155-56  (2024)  (citation omitted).  
In determining whether the language of a rule or regulation 
is mandatory or directory, common law principles of statutory 
construction apply. 
RPP § 12-801-60 states “decisions and orders . . . shall be 
rendered within forty-five days.” It is true that, generally, 
“[w]here the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous that 
a specific time provision must be met, it is mandatory and not 
merely directory.” Tataii v. Cronin, 119 Hawaiʻi 337, 339, 198 
P.3d 124, 126 (2008) (per curiam) (citation omitted). But we 
have also held that “the use of the word ‘shall’ in [a] statute 
is not dispositive of the issue of whether the statute is 
mandatory rather than directory.” Jack Endo Elec., Inc. v. Lear 
Siegler, Inc., 59 Haw. 612, 616, 585 P.2d 1265, 1269 (1978) 
(citation omitted) . The court explained: 
In  determining  whether  a  statute  is  mandatory  or  directory  
the  intention  of  the  legislature  must  be  ascertained.   The  
legislative  intent  may  be  determined  from  a  consideration  
of  the  entire  act,  its  nature,  its  object,  and  the  
consequences  that  would  result  from  construing  it  one  way  
or  the  other.   In  general,  a  statute  is  directory  rather  
than  mandatory  if  the  provisions  of  the  statute  do  not  
relate  to  the  essence  of  the  thing  to  be  done  or  where  no  
substantial  rights  depend  on  compliance  with  the  particular  
provisions  and  no  injury  can  result  from  ignoring  them.  
59 Haw. at 617, 585 P.2d at 1269 (cleaned up) (emphasis

 Critically, this court has held that “when a statute 
specifies what result will ensue if its terms are not complied 
with, the statute is deemed mandatory . . . Conversely, when a 
statute merely requires certain things to be done and nowhere 
prescribes the results that shall follow if such things are not 
done, the statute is merely directory.” Narmore v. Kawafuchi , 
112 Hawai‘i 69, 82, 143 P.3d 1271, 1284 (2006) (internal 
quotations and citation omitted) ; see also Perry v. Planning 
Comm’n of Hawaii County, 62 Haw. 666, 676, 619 P.2d 95, 103 
(1980) (“Seemingly absolute time periods for administrative 
action . . . are often considered mere guides for the conduct of 
business with dispatch and for orderly procedure. They have 
generally been characterized as directory[.]” (citation 
omitted)). 
Stated differently, a statute specifying a time within 
which public officials are required to perform an act is 
directory unless the statute denies the exercise of power 
after such time, or the nature of the act or the statutory 
language indicates that the time was intended to be a 
limitation. 
Malahoff v. Saito , 111 Hawai‘i 168, 192, 140 P.3d 401 , 425 
(2006). 
While RPP § 12 -801-60 states “decisions and orders . . . 
shall be rendered within forty -five days ,” the rule does not 
contain any consequences for non-compliance. In contrast with 
the clear consequences within HRS § 91 -13.5, which was addressed 
and properly distinguished by the ICA, the rule does not provide

 that an appeal must be approved if a decision is not issued 
within forty -five days. 
Further, in Narmore, this court identified several 
circumstances in which the word “shall” is construed as 
directory: 
First, “shall” can be read in a nonmandatory sense when a 
statute’s purpose confutes the probability of a compulsory 
statutory design. Second, “shall” will not be read as 
mandatory when unjust consequences result. Third, the word 
“shall” may be held to b e merely directory, when no 
advantage is lost, when no right is destroyed, when no 
benefit is sacrificed, either to the public or to the 
individual, by giving it that construction. 
112 Hawai‘i at 83, 143 P.3d at 1285 (c leaned up ). 
Applying Narmore, first, the lack of a consequence in the 
rule supports an interpretation that it is directory. In 
addition, the legislative purpose behind HRS § 91-12 supports an 
interpretation that the rule is directory . The statute , which 
mandated the rule, only requir es that parties be informed of 
decisions “within a reasonable time.” HRS § 91 -12. The statute 
does not impose a requirement that the decision be issued within 
forty-five days. 
Second, a mandatory reading of the 45 -day deadline would 
lead to unjust consequences . A BVA decision involving multiple 
hearing dates and significant witness testimony would have to be 
reversed if FOF/COL are not issued within forty -five days. 
Third, Norrie did not lose any advantage, suffer 
destruction of rights, or sacrifice any benefits. As further
