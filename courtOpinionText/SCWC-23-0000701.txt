   OPINION OF THE COURT BY McKENNA, J. 
I. Introduction 
During the COVID -19 pandemic , a tenant with a disability 
requested that a landlord provide an accommodation waiving a 
weekly cleaning s provision in a rental agreement. The tenant 
explained that, because of his disability, he faced an increased 
risk of hospitalization or death if he contract ed the virus . He 
therefor e did not want non-family members entering the property. 
The landlord denied the request, saying that the accommodation 
was neither reasonable nor necessary. 
The Circuit Court of the Fifth Circuit (“circuit court”) 
granted summary judgment in favor of the landlord. T he 
Intermediate Court of Appeals (“ICA”) vacated this judgment and 
ordered a remand. On certiorari, the landlord alleges the ICA 
erred. 
The ICA did not err by vacating summary judgment . But in 
this opinion, we provide guidance on how disability 
accommodation requests are to be evaluated under Hawai ʻi’s fair 
housing law. We also address an issue not addressed by the ICA . 
Although parties are encouraged to engage in an interactive 
process, under current law, there is no standalone liability if 
there is a breakdown in engagement.

II. Background 
A. Legal Background 
1. Formation of the HCRC 
The Hawai ʻi Civil Rights Commission (“HCRC”)  was formed 
through Act 219, Session Laws of Hawai ʻi  1988, which was codified 
as HRS Chapter 368. 1998  Haw. Sess. Laws  Act 219, § 1 at 1-3; 
see HRS § 368-1 (2015).  HCRC is authorized to provide “a 
uniform procedure” for enforcing Hawaiʻi’s anti-discrimination 
laws in the areas of employment (HRS Chapter 378), housing (HRS 
Chapter 515), and public accommodations (HRS Chapter 489). HRS 
§ 368-1 (2015); see  HRS § 515-9.  
In enacting Chapter 368, the Hawaiʻi State Legislature (the 
“Legislature”) found and declared, among other things, that 
discrimination in housing is contrary to public policy. Chapter 
368 also vests the HCRC with powers to “receive, investigate, 
and conciliate complaints;” “hold hearings and make inquiries;” 
“commence civil act ions in circuit court to seek appropriate 
relief;” “issue the right to sue to a complainant;” “order 
appropriate legal and equitable relief or affirmative action;” 
and “adopt rules.” HRS § 368 -3(1)-(5), (9) (2015).

 2. Hawaiʻi’s Fair Housing Law 
Hawaiʻi’s fair housing law is codified in the Hawai ʻi 
Discrimination in Real Property Transactions Act , Hawaiʻi Revised 
Statutes ( “HRS”) §§ 515 et seq. , and Hawaiʻi Administrative Rule s 
(“HAR”) §§ 12-46-301 et seq . Hawaiʻi’s fair housing law 
protects against discrimination on the basis of race, sex 
(including gender identi ty or expression), sexual orientation, 
color, religion, marital status, familial status, ancestry, 
disability, age, or human immunodeficiency virus infection 
(“HIV”). HRS § 515 -3(a) (Supp. 2023). Chapter 515 applies to 
sales, leases, rentals, and other housing practices, see HRS §§ 
515-3(a)(1)-(11) and 515 -5 (2018), and provides complainants 
with substantive rights as well as potential legal and equitable 
remedies . See HRS §§ 515-9 (2018) and 515-13 (2018). 
When Chapter 515 was initially promulgated in 1967, the 
Legislature directed  that it “shall be construed according to 
the fair import of its terms and shall be liberally construed.” 
HRS § 515-1  (2018).  
Then,  in 1992, the Legislature  passed Act 171, Session Laws 
of Hawaiʻi 1992. The purpose section of Act 171 stated:   
SECTION 1. The purpose of this Act is to conform the Real 
Estate Transactions Law, which prohibits discrimination in 
matters related to housing and real estate, with the 
federal Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988. The Department 
of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) has published notice 
that Hawai ʻi’s law is no longer substantially equivalent to 
federal law. HUD intends to decertify Hawai ʻi unless these 
amendments are enacted. Certification is necessary in order

 for Hawai ʻi to receive federal funds for the investigation 
of housing discrimination cases.  
1992 Haw. Sess. L aws Act 171 § 1 at 1 (“Act 171”) . 
The HCRC administers Hawai ʻi’s fair housing law.  To 
maintain HCRC’s federal certification, HUD requires that “the 
fair housing law that the agency administers provide [] rights, 
procedures, remedies, and the availability of judicial review 
that are substantially equivalent to those provided in the 
federal Fair Housing Act.” 24 C.F.R. § 115.201(a) & (b) ; see 
HRS Chapter 368. Act 171 was passed because Hawaiʻi’s fair 
housing law was apparently not as protective as the 1988 federal 
Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988 (“FHAA”) , especially with 
respect to disability discrimination.

As required by Act 171  of 1992, the HCRC  promulgated HAR

III. Standard of Review 
A. Motion for Summary Judgment 
An appellate court reviews an “award of summary judgment de 
novo under the same standard applied by the circuit court. ”   
Fujimoto v. Au, 95 Hawaiʻi 116, 136, 19 P.3d 699, 719 (2001)  
(emphasis removed)  (citation omitted).  
Summary judgment is appropriate if the pleadings, 
depositions, answers to interrogatories, and admissions on 
file, together with the affidavits, if any, show that there 
is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the 
moving party is entitled to ju dgment as a matter of law. 
Id.  (cleaned up). We must review the evidence in the light most 
favorable to the party opposing the motion for summary judgment. 
Fujimoto, 95 Hawai ʻi at 137, 19 P.3d at 720 (cleaned up).  
With respect to burdens : 
The burden is on the party moving for summary judgment 
(moving party) to show the absence of any genuine issue as 
to all material facts, which, under applicable principles 
of substantive law, entitles the moving party to judgment 
as a matter of law. This b urden has two components. 
First, the moving party has the burden of producing support 
for its claim that: (1) no genuine issue of material fact 
exists with respect to the essential elements of the claim 
or defense which the motion seeks to establish or which the 
motion questions; a nd (2) based on the undisputed facts, it 
is entitled to summary judgment as a matter of law. Only 
when the moving party satisfies its initial burden of 
production does the burden shift to the non -moving party to 
respond to the motion for summary judgment a nd demonstrate 
specific facts, as opposed to general allegations, that 
present a genuine issue worthy of trial. 
Second, the moving party bears the ultimate burden of 
persuasion. This burden always remains with the moving 
party and requires the moving party to convince the court 
that no genuine issue of material fact exists and that the

  moving party is entitled to summary judgment as a matter of 
law. 
French v. Hawaii Pizza Hut, Inc., 105 Hawaiʻi  462, 470, 99 P.3d 
1046,  1054  (2004)  (cleaned up).  
[Also,] a summary judgment movant may satisfy [their] 
initial burden of production by either (1) producing 
admissible evidence to show there was no genuine issue of 
material fact, or (2) showing that the non -moving party 
cannot carry [their] burden of proof at tri al. . . . [T]he

Ralston v. Yim, 129 Hawaiʻi 46, 48, 292 P.3d 1276, 1278 (2013)

B. Statutory Interpretation 
Our standard of review for statutory interpretation is 
well-established. “The interpretation of a statute is a 
question of law which [the appellate] court reviews de novo.” 
Sierra Club v. Dep ’t of Transp., 120 Hawaiʻi  181, 197, 202 P.3d 
1226, 1242 (2009) (cleaned up). Our construction of statutes is 
guided by the following principles:  
First, the fundamental starting point for statutory -
interpretation  is the language of the statute itself. 
Second, where the statutory language is plain and 
unambiguous, our sole duty is to give effect to its plain 
and obvious meaning. Third, implicit in the task of statutory construction is our foremost obligation to 
ascertain and give effect to the intention of the 
legislature, which is to be obtained primarily from the 
language contained in the statute itself. Fourth, when 
there is doubt, doubleness of  meaning, or indistinctiveness 
or uncertainty of an expression used in a statute, an 
ambiguity exists.  
When there is ambiguity in a statute, “the meaning of 
the ambiguous words may be sought by examining the context 
with which the ambiguous words, phrases, and sentences may be compared, in order to ascertain their true meaning.” 
Moreover, the courts may resort to extrinsic aids in 
12   *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI ‘I REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
 
 
  
 
      
  
 
   
 
 determining legislative intent, such as legislative 
history, or the reason and spirit of the law. 
State v. Wheeler, 121 Hawaiʻi 383, 390, 219 P.3d 1170, 1177 
(2009) (cleaned up).

B. Under HRS § 515 -3(a)(9), plaintiffs bear the initial burden

Howard v. HMK Holdings, LLC , 988 F.3d 1185 (9th Cir. 2021)

a prima facie case of discrimination based on a failure to 
accommodate , a plaintiff must demonstrate : 
(1) that the plaintiff  .  .  . is [disabled ]  within the

should reasonably be expected to [have]  know[n]  of the

practices, or services] of the [disability ]  may be 
necessary to afford the [person with the disability ]  an

and enjoy the dwelling; (4) that the accommodation [in 
rules, policies, practices, or services] is reasonable; and 
(5) that defendant refused to make the requested accommodation.

  Although HRS § 515 -2, HAR § 12 -46-302, and 42 U.S.C. § 3602 
do not define “necessary,” “equal opportunity,” or “reasonable,” 
various federal courts have addressed these terms under the FHA. 
We adopt the following definitions and standards for these 
terms.   
Starting with “necessary,” some courts have observed that 
“it means ‘[i]ndispensable, requisite, essential, [and] needful” 
and conveys “a high standard,” Vorchheimer v. Philadelphian 
Owners Ass’n, 903 F.3d 100, 105 (3d Cir. 2018), while others 
have noted, “if the proposed accommodation provides no direct 
amelioration of a disability’s effect, it cannot be said to be 
‘necessary.’”   Bryant Woods Inn, Inc. v. Howard Cnty., Md., 124 
F.3d 597, 604 (4th Cir. 1997)  (citation omitted). See  Cinnamon 
Hills Youth Crisis Ctr., Inc. v. Saint George City, 685 F.3d

D. There is no standalone liability for failure to engage in 
an “interactive process” under HRS § 515 -3(a)(9). 
Finally, a lthough the ICA did not reach the issue, Parrish 
and the HCRC urge us to address whether HRS § 515 -3(a)(9) 
requires housing providers to engage in an “interactive process” 
and whether failure to do so gives rise to standalone liability. 
We hold that neither H RS § 515 -3(a)(9) nor HAR § 12 -46-306(a)(1) 
and (3) require housing providers to engage in an interactive 
process. Therefore, there can be no standalone liability on 
that basis. 
We begin our analysis with the text of the statute. 
Neither HRS § 515 -3(a)(9) nor 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(B) mentions 
“interactive process,” nor is it referenced elsewhere in those 
statutes. A plain reading of HRS § 515 -3(a)(9) suggests it 
encourages housi ng providers to engage with tenants by 
requesting verifying information when a “disability” or 
“disability -related need for an assistance animal is not readily 
apparent,” while at the same time limiting certain inquiries.
