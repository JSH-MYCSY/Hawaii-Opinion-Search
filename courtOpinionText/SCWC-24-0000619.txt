III. STANDARDS OF REVIEW 
A. Plain Error 
We may recognize plain error when the error committed 
affects substantial rights. In re N.C., 123 Hawai‘ i 184, 193, 
231 P.3d 457, 466 (2010) (citations omitted). 
B. Constitutional Questions 
“We review questions of constitutional law de novo, under 
the right/wrong standard.” In Interest of FG, AG, PG, 142 
Hawai‘i 497, 503, 421 P.3d 1267, 1273 (2018) (emphasis and 
citations omitted). 
IV. DISCUSSION 
A. Uchima applies to parental rights termination proceedings. “As a general rule, compliance with the requirement of 
timely filing of a notice of appeal is jurisdictional, and we 
must dismiss an appeal on our motion if we lack jurisdiction.” 
State v. Knight, 80 Hawai‘ i 318, 323, 909 P.2d 1133, 1138 (1996) 
HRS § 587A- 20 (2018) provides: “The court may order that testimony or 
other evidence produced by a party in a proceeding under this chapter shall 
be inadmissible as evidence in any other state civil or criminal action or 
proceeding if the court deems such an order to be in the best interests of 
the child.” HRS § 587A- 20. 
9 6  *** FOR PUBLICATION IN WEST’S HAWAI I REPORTS AND PACIFIC REPORTER *** 
 
 
 (cleaned up). Nevertheless, “we have permitted belated appeals 
under certain circumstances, namely, when defense counsel has 
inexcusably or ineffectively failed to pursue a defendant’s 
appeal from a criminal conviction in the first instance.” Id.
(cleaned up). 
In Uchima, for instance, we declined to dismiss a criminal 
defendant’s writ application that was filed six days after it 
was due. 147 Hawaiʻ i at 72-83, 464 P.3d at 860-71.  We noted 
that “certiorari review is a critical stage of the criminal 
proceedings during which a defendant has the constitutional 
right to effective assistance of counsel, which includes counsel’s procedural compliance with the steps required to 
timely file an application for a writ of certiorari.” Id. at 
69, 464 P.3d at 857. Accordingly, we held that “this court may decline to dismiss an application for writ of certiorari as 
untimely and proceed to review its merits when it is plain from 
the record that defense counsel failed to comply with the 
procedural requirements for filing the application.” Id. at 82, 
464 P.3d at 870 (citation omitted). 
We later extended our holding in Uchima to include juvenile 
law-violators’ proceedings in In the Interest of GH
, 152 Hawaiʻi 
8, 518 P.3d 1158 (2022). There, we rendered a merits

 determination despite defense counsel filing a writ application 
more than eight months after the ICA filed its judgment on 
appeal. Id. at 14-15, 518 P.3d at 1164-65. 
We recognize that the present appeal is not criminal in 
nature. However, we have held that “parents have a 
constitutional right to counsel under article I, section 5 [of 
the Hawaiʻ i Constitution] in parental termination proceedings[.]”  
In re T.M., 131 Hawai‘ i 419, 421, 319 P.3d 338, 340 (2014). See
also Interest of P Children, 155 Hawaiʻ i 332, 337, 564 P.3d 1131, 
1136 (2025) (“The Hawai‘ i Constitution’s due process clause, 
article I, section 5, confers parents a right to counsel in 
Child Protective Act proceedings.” (cleaned up)). 
We thus 
extend Uchima
 to parental rights termination proceedings. In 
such cases, we may decline to dismiss an application for writ of certiorari as untimely. 
Here, Father’s writ application was filed on July 17, 2025, 
more than thirty days after the ICA filed its Judgment on Appeal

It is therefore untimely. See Hawaiʻ i Rules

 B. Whether the Service Plan violates Article I, Section 10 and 
the Fifth Amendment is appropriate for plain error review. 
Father argues for the first time in his certiorari

right to free speech. Additionally, no party raised whether the 
Service Plan violated Father’s constitutional right against self-incrimination. We first identified that issue in our order

As explained below, we hold that the Service Plan violates

v. Murphy, 465 U.S. 420, 426 (1984) (citing Lefkowitz v. Turley, 
414 U.S. 70, 77 (1973)). 
The privilege against self-incrimination “is not limited to 
an accused testifying at [their] own criminal trial, but applies 
to testimony of any witness at any proceeding, where the 
testimony might tend to show that the witness had committed a 
crime.” Kupihea, 80 Hawai‘i at 313, 909 P.2d at 1128.  Indeed, 
while the privilege is limited to criminal matters, “it is as 
broad as the mischief against which it seeks to guard.” Id.
In this respect, 
Matter of Welfare of J.W., 415 N.W.2d 879 
(Minn. 1987), which involved a court-ordered treatment plan 
requiring parents to make incriminating disclosures, is 
informative. The trial court there found that either or both 
parents were responsible for the death of their nephew during a 
trial held to determine if the two children of the parents were 
“dependent and neglected[.]” Id. at 880-81. The parents’ 
children were placed in foster care due to a high risk of 
physical abuse in the family home. Id. at 881. 
The trial court entered an order adopting the county-

 recommended family rehabilitation program, which included 
psychological evaluations requiring “the explanation of the 
death of [the nephew], consistent with the medical findings.” 
Id. Before the trial court, the state’s attorney indicated that 
if the parents refused to undergo the psychological evaluations as ordered, the state would file a petition for parental 
termination. Id.
On appeal, the Minnesota Supreme Court found the treatment 
plan unconstitutional. Id. at 883. As an initial matter, the court held that the parents’ Fifth Amendment challenge was not 
premature, rejecting the state’s ripeness argument. Id. at 882-
83. The court reasoned that even though a termination petition 
had not been filed, the state’s “threat” of termination was 
“genuine, direct, and immediate,” and such a penalty was a 
“potent sanction.” Id. at 882. The court also noted that 
“termination of parental rights would not be automatic if the parents refuse to cooperate with the court-ordered treatment 
plan, but that refusal would be a substantial, relevant factor 
in determining whether or not parental rights should be 
terminated.” Id. at 882-83. Because this amounted to an 
impermissible penalty for invoking the privilege against self-
incrimination, the court found the trial court’s order

  unenforceable to the extent it required the parents to 
incriminate themselves. Id. at 883; see also Matter of A.D.L., 
402 P.3d 1280, 1285 (Nev. 2017) (agreeing “with other courts that have held that a parent may not be compelled to admit a 
crime under the threat of the loss of parental rights”); In re

parents to otherwise undergo treatment, but it may not 
specifically require an admission of guilt as part of the 
treatment.”). 
Here, the family court’s order implementing the Service 
Plan lodges a “genuine, direct, and immediate” threat of parental termination. See J.W., 415 N.W.2d at 882. As stated,

Service Plan. If Father does not receive a clinical discharge,

with a safe family home[.]” See HRS § 587A-7(a)(8). The family

home with the assistance of a service plan within a reasonable

 shall be subject to termination, pursuant to law.” This threat

883; see also State v. Zuffante, 157 Hawai‘ i 194, 203, 576 P.3d 
243, 252 (2025) (“This illusory choice — either remain silent 
and let decisive evidence go unchallenged, or testify to refute

The Service Plan is unconstitutional to the extent it 
requires Father’s admission of sexual abuse. The State may 
mandate therapy, but it may not go as far as requiring Father to incriminate himself, particularly given that he has maintained 
his innocence. See State v. Reyes, 93 Hawai‘ i 321, 328-29, 2

participation in [the statutorily mandated sex offender 
treatment program], including admitting his sex crime(s).”).

2. The privilege against self-incrimination does not 
protect against the adverse consequences of failing to 
undergo effective therapy. 
Although the State may not require incriminating 
disclosures as the Service Plan does, we note that therapy which 
does not require admission to past offenses may not be 
effective, “and ineffective therapy may hurt the parents’ 
chances of regaining their children.” J.W., 415 N.W.2d at 883. 
Considering these competing concerns, the J.W. court held 
that the Fifth Amendment does not protect parents from the consequences of failing to undergo meaningful therapy, 
particularly where the refusal to admit to past abuse hinders 
engagement. Id. at 884. That court explained: 
In the lexicon of the Fifth Amendment, the risk of losing 
the children for failure to undergo meaningful therapy is 
neither a “threat” nor a “penalty” imposed by the state. 
It is simply a consequence of the reality that it is unsafe 
for children to be with parents who are abusive and 
violent.
